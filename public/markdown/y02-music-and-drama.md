PRIME BOOKS · CONTINUED

**Rain, Rain, Go Away, English. First published 17th century or earlier. A true SO-MI song: the**
whole tune sits on two notes, which is why it opens the pitch work in this book. Recorded from the
17th century or earlier, so the text is long out of copyright. Set from the common oral version,
which is the two lines above and nothing more; the longer variants are later additions and are not
printed.

**Star Light, Star Bright, American, sung throughout Britain. First published 1880s. Late**
nineteenth century and comfortably out of copyright. Another two-note so-mi chant in most
classroom versions, which is why it appears in Unit 1 and returns in Unit 4 with the night songs.

**Bee, Bee, Bumble Bee, English playground rhyme. First published traditional. A traditional**
playground counting-out chant with no known author, sung on so and mi. Set from the common
oral version. It is printed because a so-mi unit needs more than one song a class can already
almost sing.

**Snail, Snail, English playground rhyme. First published traditional. A traditional two-note game**
song, author unknown, set from the common oral version. Its whole melody is so and mi, and its
words are slow, which makes it the song Tessa the tortoise leads.

PRIME BOOKS · CONTINUED

**Row, Row, Row Your Boat, American, sung throughout Britain. First published 1852. First printed**
in 1852 and long out of copyright. It is in this unit because it is the round every Year 2 class can
manage, and a round is the simplest true experience of layering.

**A Sailor Went to Sea, English clapping song. First published traditional. A traditional clapping**
song with no known author, set from the common oral version. Printed here for its steady pulse
and its repeated words, which is the pairing the water unit needs before the soundscapes.

**Hot Cross Buns, English street cry. First published 1798 or earlier. An eighteenth-century London**
street cry, printed by the 1790s and long out of copyright. The most useful food song in the book:
its words fall into ta and ti-ti exactly, so the rhythm work in topic 3.2 can be done on a song the
class already knows.

**Pat-a-cake, Pat-a-cake, English. First published 1698. One of the oldest recorded English nursery**
rhymes, appearing in a play of 1698. Set from the common oral version. Its syllables are so regular
that it doubles as the clapping game for the pulse work.

**Polly Put the Kettle On, English. First published 1803. Printed from 1803 and out of copyright.**
The repeated line is an ostinato in everything but name, which is why it sits beside topic 3.3.

**Pease Porridge Hot, English. First published 1760. Recorded from about 1760 and out of**
copyright. A clapping game whose text is built out of food words, which is precisely what topic 3.2
asks for.

**Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star, English words, French tune. First published 1806. The words are**
Jane Taylor's poem The Star, published in 1806, and the tune is the older French melody Ah! vous
dirai-je, maman. Both are out of copyright. Mozart wrote variations on the tune and did not
compose it, so his name is not attached to it here.

**I See the Moon, English. First published traditional. A traditional rhyme with no known author, set**
from the common oral version. In the book it is offered as the quiet song for the dark half of the
Turning Day, and a class may sing it or say it.

**When the Saints Go Marching In, American gospel. First published 1896 (early published forms).**
A gospel hymn whose early published forms date from the 1890s, out of copyright. Printed here
because its four lines repeat, so a whole class can learn it in one session and add percussion parts
to it in the next.

**Auld Lang Syne, Scots, Robert Burns. First published 1788. Robert Burns set this down in 1788**
and it has been out of copyright for well over a century. The first verse and chorus only: that is
what a class actually sings, and it is the song the book ends the year on.

**It's Raining, It's Pouring, American. First published 1912 (first two lines), recorded 1939. NOT**
PRINTED. The obvious rain song for this unit, and the reason this file exists. Its first two lines are
traceable only to The Little Mother Goose, published in the United States in 1912, and the rhyme
as a whole is recorded from 1939. That is far too recent for a priced book to reproduce, however
old the tune feels. Teachers may of course sing it with a class, and the book names it so they can.

**Kum Ba Yah, African American spiritual, disputed. First published earliest known recording 1926.**
TUNE ONLY, WORDS NOT PRINTED. The authorship is genuinely disputed, with rival claims and an
earliest known recording of 1926. Where authorship is contested, the safe reading is that
somebody may hold a claim, so the melody may be sung and the text is not reproduced here.

**Happy Birthday to You, American. First published melody 1893, familiar text later. NOT**
PRINTED. The most famous rights history in music: the familiar text was subject to a disputed
claim for decades, settled in the United States in 2015 and 2016, and the position still differs
between territories. A school singing it in a classroom and a publisher printing it in a priced book
are not in the same position, and this book is the publisher.

**For He's a Jolly Good Fellow, English. First published tune 18th century French, English text later.**
TUNE ONLY. The melody is the old French Malbrough s'en va-t-en guerre and is out of copyright,
but the familiar English text has no single clear pre-1930 published source that could be verified,
so it is not printed.

GETTING SET UP · CONTINUED

One raised paw: start, and stop.

Where to stand

## In a circle, if you can. In a circle everybody can see everybody, which

matters enormously the moment two groups have to play different

things at the same time.

## Keep one clear space in the middle. That is where whoever is

performing stands, and it is where Badger stands when he is counting

you in.

✎FOR TEACHERS

Two agreed hand signals are worth more than any amount of asking for quiet: one
for START and one for STOP. Teach them in the first lesson and use nothing else all
year. Pupils who know they will be stopped cleanly play far more freely.

A taped circle on the floor and a taped square in the middle of it will save you
several minutes in every single lesson, and it makes the difference between two
groups layering their parts and two groups shouting over each other.

Where a topic asks for five tuned notes, use C, D, E, G and A and remove the others
if your instrument allows it. Taking F and B away is not a simplification: it is what
makes it impossible for a pupil to play a wrong note, which is the whole reason the
scheme names a pentatonic set.

## ♫YOUR VOICE HAS MORE THAN ONE SETTING

You already know this without being told. You do not talk to a baby

the way you shout across a playground, and you do not whisper a

## ♫A WHISPER DOES NOT BUZZ

Put two fingers gently on the front of your throat. Say your own

name out loud. Feel that? That is buzzing.

Now whisper your name. The buzz stops completely. A whisper is

## not a small voice, it is a different voice, made without the buzzing

part at all.

## ♪OTTO'S TRUE THING

You sing with your throat, but a bird does not. A bird has a special
sound box called a syrinx, deep in its chest where its windpipe splits in
two. That is why some birds can sing two notes at the same time.

## ♫COPYING IS HOW YOU LEARN A SOUND

You cannot get better at a sound by being told about it. You get

## better by hearing it and having a go, then hearing it again and

having another go.

## ♫THE PULSE IS THE STEADY ONE UNDERNEATH

## The pulse is the plain, even counting that goes on underneath

music. It does not get excited. It does not slow down at the sad

## ♫FIVE NOTES INSTEAD OF SEVEN

A normal set of notes has seven in it before it starts again. This

## one has five. Two of them, f and b, have been taken out.

## The proper name for a set of five notes is a pentatonic scale. Penta

is an old Greek word for five.

## ♫WHY TAKING NOTES AWAY HELPS

It sounds backwards, but it is true: with these five notes, you

cannot play a wrong one. Any order works. Any two together work.

## That means you can concentrate entirely on when to play, which is

## the hard part, instead of worrying about what to play.

## ♫PUTTING THE TUNE ON THE RHYTHM

You already know how to make a four-beat rhythm. Now put a note

on each sound of it, choosing from the five. That is a real melody,

and you made it.

Some music sounds like sunshine and some sounds like a dark

## ♫POINTING COMES BEFORE EXPLAINING

## ♫FOUR BEATS IS A BOX YOU FILL

A bar of four beats is a box. You can put anything you like in it as

## long as it adds up to four.

One ta is one beat. One ti-ti is one beat, with two sounds in it. One

## ♫EMPTY BEATS ARE THE BEST ONES

Beginners fill every beat, and it turns into a blur. Leave a beat

## empty and the audience leans in.

A rhythm with a silence in the middle of it is nearly always more

interesting than one without.

## ♫THE RUNNING ORDER IS THE PLAN

## A running order is a list of what happens, in order, from walking

Songs we did not print, and why

Some songs are not printed in this book, and it is worth knowing why.

A song belongs to whoever made it up. If somebody wrote the words in

the last hundred years or so, then printing them in a book you can buy

is not ours to do, even when everybody already knows the song.

Your teacher can still sing all of these with you. Singing a song and

printing it in a book are two different things.

## 1912 (first two lines), recorded 1939. American. NOT PRINTED.

recorded from 1939. That is far too recent for a priced book to

reproduce, however old the tune feels. Teachers may of course

Every "Otto's true thing" in this book was checked against a named source before it was

Unit 1: The Dawn Chorus

**The Dawn Chorus: You sing with your throat, but a bird does not. A bird has a special sound box**
called a syrinx, deep in its chest where its windpipe splits in two. That is why some birds can sing
two notes at the same time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrinx_(bird_anatomy)

**The Dawn Chorus: Very early in the morning, birds all start singing at once. It is called the dawn**
chorus, and different kinds of bird join in at different times, so the chorus changes while it goes on.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawn_chorus_(birds)

**The Dawn Chorus: Birds sing most at the very start of the day, when the air is still and the world**
is quiet. A song travels better through calm quiet air than through a busy noisy morning.
**https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawn_chorus_(birds)What we did not say: The widely repeated**
claim that dawn song carries twenty times further than midday song has no primary measurement
behind it, so no number is printed here.

**The Dawn Chorus: A singing bird is usually doing one of two jobs: telling other birds that this**
hedge is taken, or looking for a mate. It is a message, not just a nice noise.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_vocalization

**The Dawn Chorus: Robins will sing in the middle of the night if there is a street light near their**
tree. They are not confused: light is one of the things that tells a robin it is time to sing.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_robin

Unit 2: The Stream

**The Stream: Sound travels more than four times faster through water than through air. If you put**
your ear against the side of a full bath and tap it, the sound arrives sooner than you expect.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound

**The Stream: Whales sing to each other under the sea. Sound carries a very long way through**
water, much further than it does through air, so a whale can call to another whale it cannot see at
**all. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_vocalizationWhat we did not say: No distance figure is**
given. The often-quoted thousands of miles depends on conditions and is not a number a child can
use.

**The Stream: Rain does not make one sound. Rain on a leaf, rain on a window, rain on a puddle and**
rain on a roof are four different sounds, all made by the same water.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain

**The Stream: A composer called Smetana wrote a piece of music about a real river, the Vltava. It**
begins with two tiny streams played by two flutes, and they join together into one big tune, just as
two streams join into a river. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vltava_(Smetana)

**The Stream: Chopin never called his famous piece Raindrop. Somebody else gave it that name**
later, because one note keeps repeating all the way through it, like a drip that will not stop.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude,_Op._28,_No._15_(Chopin)

**The Stream: In Saint-Saens's Aquarium, the shimmering water sound is made by a glass**
harmonica, an instrument played by rubbing wet fingers on glass.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Carnival_of_the_Animals

**The Stream: If you fill bottles with different amounts of water and tap them, each one sings a**
different note. The more water there is, the lower the note goes.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_harp

**The Stream: Frogs call together in a chorus, and they take turns. If they all called at exactly the**
same moment, no frog could be heard on its own. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frog

**The Stream: Dolphins make clicking sounds and then listen for the echo coming back. The echo**
tells them what is in front of them, even in water too murky to see through.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_echolocation

Unit 3: The Long Table

**The Long Table: You have been keeping a steady beat since before you were born. Your heart does**
it all day and all night without being asked, and it does not speed up just because the music does.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_rate

**The Long Table: Every word is already made of beats. We call them syllables, and you can hear**
them by clapping a word: ap-ple has two claps and ba-na-na has three.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable

**The Long Table: A pentatonic scale has five notes instead of seven. Two notes have been taken**
away on purpose, and that is what makes it so hard to play anything that sounds wrong.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentatonic_scale

**The Long Table: A pattern that repeats and repeats underneath a piece of music has a proper**
name: an ostinato. The word comes from an old Italian word meaning stubborn, which is exactly
what it is. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostinato

**The Long Table: The words ta and ti-ti are not made up. They come from a way of teaching music**
that a Hungarian composer called Zoltan Kodaly helped to spread, and children all over the world
chant them. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method

**The Long Table: The crunch you hear when you eat a crisp is much louder inside your own head**
than it is to anybody else. The sound travels straight through the bones of your skull to your ears.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_conduction

**The Long Table: Pulse and rhythm are not the same thing. The pulse is the steady counting**
underneath, and the rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds you put on top of it.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_(music)

**The Long Table: The word xylophone means wood sound. A xylophone has wooden bars, and the**
longer the bar, the lower the note it makes. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylophone

**The Long Table: People have always sung while doing hard jobs together, like hauling ropes or**
grinding corn. The song keeps everybody pulling at the same moment, which makes the work
easier. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_song

Unit 4: The Turning Day

**The Turning Day: Music built on a minor scale usually strikes listeners as darker or sadder than**
music built on a major one. Almost everybody agrees about the feeling, even though it is only a
**small change in the notes. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_scaleWhat we did not say: No**

**The Turning Day: There is a whole kind of music named after the night. It is called a nocturne,**
and it is usually quiet, slow and thoughtful, the way the world is when it is dark.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturne

**The Turning Day: An owl's feathers have soft fringed edges, so an owl can fly almost silently. It is**
not being polite: it needs to hear the mouse, and its own wings would drown the mouse out.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owl

**The Turning Day: Bats hunt in the dark by shouting and listening. Most of the shouts are too high**
for you to hear at all, which is why a bat seems silent when it is actually being very loud.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_echolocation

**The Turning Day: The letters p and f in music are short for Italian words: piano meaning soft and**
forte meaning strong. Musicians have been writing them for hundreds of years.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_(music)

**The Turning Day: The piano is named after being quiet. Its full old name meant soft and loud,**
because unlike the instruments before it, you could play it both ways just by changing your fingers.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano

**The Turning Day: Moths come out at night and are drawn to a light, while butterflies fly in the**
day. They are close relatives who chose opposite halves of the day.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moth

**The Turning Day: The words of Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star were written as a poem by Jane**
Taylor in 1806. The tune is an older French one, and the two were put together afterwards.
**https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twinkle,_Twinkle,_Little_StarWhat we did not say: Mozart did not**

left out.

**The Turning Day: The nightingale is named for singing after dark. Most birds stop when the light**
goes, but this one carries on into the night. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_nightingale

**The Big Rock: In Britain, church bells are rung in careful patterns rather than tunes. It is called**
change ringing, each bell is pulled by a different person, and everybody has to count.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_ringing

**The Big Rock: Clapping to say thank you is very old indeed. Audiences in ancient Rome clapped,**
and so did audiences long before that. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applause

**The Big Rock: A conductor makes no sound at all. Their whole job is to keep everybody together**
and to show how loud, how fast and how gentle the music should be, using only their hands.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conducting

**The Big Rock: A big orchestra can have around a hundred players in it, all playing at the same**
moment and all watching one person. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestra

**The Big Rock: Beethoven wrote his most famous celebration music when he was almost completely**
deaf. He could not hear the choir that sang it.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_(Beethoven)

**The Big Rock: In Indonesia, a whole village orchestra of tuned metal instruments is called a**
gamelan. Nobody owns their own instrument: the set belongs to everybody together.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan

**The Big Rock: Japanese taiko drums can be taller than the person playing them, and the players**
move their whole bodies. Watching them is as much a part of it as listening.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiko

**The Big Rock: Saint-Saens would not let his Carnival of the Animals be published while he was**
alive, in case people thought he was not a serious composer. It is now the piece he is most famous
for. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Carnival_of_the_Animals

**The Big Rock: When a group of people sing together, their breathing falls into step with the music**
and with each other. Singing in a choir is something your whole body joins in with.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir

**The Big Rock: Harmony is what happens when two different notes sound at the same moment. One**
person cannot make harmony alone, which is why it belongs at the end of this book.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony

Notation follows the scheme exactly. Units 1 and 2 are pre-notation and work in so

Every rhythm, ostinato, layered part and melody printed in this book was checked
by software before it was typeset: bars must fill their time signature, layered parts
must share a bar length, a two-line stave may carry only so and mi, and a
pentatonic part may not contain F or B. A pattern that does not add up cannot
appear on these pages.
