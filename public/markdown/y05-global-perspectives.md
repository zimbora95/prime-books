▸LET US FIND OUT

Think of communication as an equation, not a single word:

words + tone + body language + listening = a message that can be understood

Tone is how the voice sounds: kind, sharp, rushed, calm.

Body language is face, hands and posture.

Listening is the other half of the equation. A brilliant sentence is wasted if nobody is taking it
in.

If any part is missing, the message can go wrong. In a Lisbon playground, "Come here" can mean
join the game or stop that now, depending on the voice and the hands. A nod in class may mean "I
understand" or "I am still thinking".

Good communicators check: "Did I hear you right?"

∎WATCH ME WORK ONE OUT

Mateo says, "I'm fine," with a smile and open hands. Jamal says the same words with folded arms
and a flat voice.

Step 1. The words match. Step 2. The tone and body do not. Step 3. A careful listener asks a
check question instead of guessing.

The equation failed on tone and body, not on spelling.

▸LET US FIND OUT

When people do not share a language, they build bridges. A bridge is a fair tool so both sides can
be heard. It is not a test of who is "clever".

Useful bridges:

gestures that both people can guess

drawings and objects (a boat, a tree, a cup)

simple shared words, spoken slowly

patience and a second try

a school interpreter or a trusted adult when the message matters

Portugal is a good place to notice bridges. New classmates, visitors and families from
Portuguese-speaking countries overseas may share Portuguese, or they may not. Shouting the
same sentence louder is not a bridge. It is only noise.

A welcome buddy who points the way to lunch is a human bridge. A translation app can help, but
it needs adult care: it can guess wrongly, and it should not store a classmate's private details.

Pictures can carry meaning when words do not match.

A welcome buddy is a human language bridge.

1.3

Cultural codes

↺DO YOU REMEMBER?

Does everyone in your family greet people the same way?

◉BOOTS ON!

Stand up. Show a greeting you know: a wave, a handshake mime, a small bow of the head. No
touching if you prefer not to.

▸LET US FIND OUT

A cultural code is an unwritten habit about how people speak and behave: how close to stand,
how direct to be, whether interrupting is rude, how long to wait before answering.

Codes are not "right" or "wrong" like a maths sum. They are patterns people learn at home, in
class and in their town.

In some homes, children speak only when invited. In others, lively overlap is normal.

In Portugal you may kiss cheeks in family settings. In other places a small bow or a handshake
is preferred.

A quiet style can still be clear. A lively style can still be kind.

The skill is notice, ask, respect. Mocking a style is not curiosity. Matching your style to the
moment is part of the same skill: a library needs a quieter voice than a football pitch, even if both
voices belong to you.

1.5

The art of persuasion

↺DO YOU REMEMBER?

When did someone convince you with a good reason, not by shouting?

◉BOOTS ON!

In thirty seconds, persuade a partner to try a healthy snack using only two reasons. No nagging.

▸LET US FIND OUT

Persuasion means inviting someone to agree using reasons, stories and evidence they can check.
It is how ideas travel across a class, a town and the world.

It is not the same as:

pressure: threats, shame, endless nagging

tricks: false claims, hidden facts, "everyone is doing it" with no proof

Fair persuasion asks three questions:

1. What is my claim?

2. What is my evidence?

3. Who might disagree, and why?

Year 5 pupils can share ideas in a class assembly, a poster or a short film script. The claim still needs
facts. A loud colour is not evidence.

∎WATCH ME WORK ONE OUT

Claim: Our class should collect clean paper for recycling. Evidence: The bin by the art sink fills by
Thursday. Paper can be made into new sheets. Respectful ask: Please bring spare paper on
Monday. Counter-view: Someone might say the caretaker already sorts it. Reply: Then we can
ask the caretaker what would actually help, before we print a poster.

▸LET US FIND OUT

Active listening means listening to understand, not only to reply.

Tools:

a quiet body

eyes if that is comfortable (some people listen better looking slightly aside)

no interrupting

say-it-back: repeat the idea in your own words and check

open questions: How… Why… What happened next…

Closed questions need yes or no. Open questions need a reason. "Did you like it?" is closed.
"What surprised you?" is open.

In class discussion, active listening is how different perspectives get a fair turn. In later units
(laws, work, other countries) you will need the same skill so you do not jump to a stereotype.

Listening is a skill. It can be practised like a sport.

Say-it-back shows you listened to understand.

Open questions invite a story, not only yes or no.

Laws are formal rules made by a community or country so people can live together with safety,
fairness and predictability. Traffic laws, food safety laws and school policies are different layers
of the same idea: shared order.

debate and update them. For Year 5, the key is: laws are for people, including people who will
never meet each other.

▸LET US FIND OUT

A right is something you are entitled to because you are a person in a community (for example, to
learn in safety). A responsibility (duty) is something you should do to protect others' rights (for
example, not blocking a fire exit).

Rights without responsibilities become selfish. Responsibilities without rights become harsh.
Good communities hold both.

Example: the right to speak in class discussion is paired with the duty to listen and not shout over
others.

▸LET US FIND OUT

Human rights are basic entitlements every person should have because they are human: dignity,
safety, education, health care, freedom from cruel treatment, and a fair process when accused.

After the Second World War, countries wrote the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)
to set a shared standard. Children's rights are also described in later agreements, including the
Convention on the Rights of the Child.

Human rights are not pocket money for some countries only. They are a promise to everyone,
including children who never leave their town.

▸LET US FIND OUT

When people break laws, communities use consequences: warnings, fines, community service,
or, for serious adult crimes, courts and prisons. For children, systems usually aim to educate and
protect, not to copy adult punishment.

Fair consequences try to: stop the harm, protect others, and help the person repair damage
where possible. Unfair consequences are random, cruel or applied only to some groups.

This topic is not about naming classmates. It is about principles.
