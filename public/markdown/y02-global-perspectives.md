In Ghana, children play oware on a wooden board with two rows of six holes

Hand on your chest. Can you feel it? That is your heart doing its job.

Put your hand flat on your chest and sit still. Now run on the spot for twenty

## Playing safely

**DO YO U REMEMBER?**

Why do we walk, and not run, when the floor is wet?

**BO O TS O N!**

Look at the helmet in the picture. Show with your fingers where it should

**LET US FIND OUT**

Nobody makes rules just to spoil your fun. Almost every rule in a game is
there because somebody once got hurt.

A helmet must sit level on your head, two finger widths above your
eyebrows, with the straps making a V under your ears. If it is tipped back it
protects nothing at all.

And you warm up first. Gentle running, then stretching, then the game. Cold
muscles tear. Warm muscles bend.

Have you ever been in a team? What was your job in it?

**BO O TS O N!**

Stand in a line of five. Everybody clap at the same moment. Not nearly

**LET US FIND OUT**

A team is not just several people. It is several people who need each other.

In volleyball there are six players, and every time the team wins the serve
back, everybody moves round one place. That means nobody can hog the
serving, ever. The rule builds turn taking right into the game.

In tug of war, one very strong person cannot win on their own. The team has
to pull at exactly the same moment. It was even an Olympic sport once.

# Time capsule

## History

Children in ancient Egypt ran and jumped and wrestled. Children in ancient China kicked a ball called cuju, which was a little bit like football. In Ghana children played ampe, clapping and jumping. In India they played kabaddi, and in Portugal the old game of the péla was chased down the street.

Nobody bought these games in a shop. Children taught them to each other, and they travelled from land to land the way a ball travels from player to player.

## Things we did not print

Everything in this book was checked before it was printed. Three things
were checked and then left out, and it is worth knowing why.

## A photograph of a real child in a real ger. We could not find one whose
family had clearly agreed to it being printed in a school book, so Expedition
2 carries a painting and a drawn outline instead. Rule five: nobody is a
stranger's story.

## A number for how many people in the world have no clean water. The
figure changes every year and the two sources we trusted did not agree with
each other, so topic 2.1 teaches what a need is and sends you to a live page
for the number instead of freezing a wrong one in print.

## A code to a video of a talking drum being played by a named musician. The
video was excellent and the page around it carried advertisements aimed at
adults, which breaks the promise on page 2 that every square is free and
asks for nothing. Topic 4.5 points at the encyclopaedia article instead.

## For teachers

This is a Student Book. It is written to the pupil and it is meant to be written in. This page is the only
one addressed to you.

**What the book assumes. Six and seven year olds who can read simple sentences with help, and**
who cannot yet write at length. Every extended answer in the book is one or two ruled lines, and
every task can be answered by drawing, circling or ticking if writing is the barrier.

**How an expedition runs. Six expeditions, two a term. Each opens with a painting and a big**
question that has no answer in the book, then runs eleven, six, eight, seven, eight and seven topics.
Each topic is one teaching page: look at the plate, read Let us find out together, do Your turn, argue
about From another chair, write the field note. Each expedition closes with a class project, a self-
check table, a Now I can checklist and its own word list.

**The big question is not rhetorical. It is the assessed skill in Global Perspectives at this stage: hold**
two accounts of the same thing at once without collapsing them into one. From another chair is
where that is practised. Rufus and Nella are never corrected, and neither should the class be pushed
to a verdict.

**The three terms. Expeditions 1 and 2 in the autumn, 3 and 4 in the spring, 5 and 6 in the summer,**
each term closing with a revision expedition, five assessment items and four activities out of the
classroom. The assessment items are not a test paper: they are the same five verbs the book
teaches, which are sort, name, explain, draw and argue.

**The little squares. Every code was opened and read on 3 August 2026. Every destination is free,**
asks for no name and shows no advertising aimed at children. Open each one yourself before the
lesson: the web changes and this book does not. Every address is printed in full in Every QR code in
this book, so a class with no device loses nothing.

**Marking. The field notes cannot be marked wrong. They are evidence of looking, not of knowing.**
The Answers section covers only the items that have an answer; everything else is marked by asking
the child to say more.

**Differentiation. A pupil who cannot yet write can dictate a field note or draw it. A pupil who wants**
more should be sent to One step further, which appears wherever a topic has somewhere further to
go.
