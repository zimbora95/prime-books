WELCOME

The Hedgerow Theatre

There is a gap in the hedge at the bottom of the field. Somebody,

nobody knows who, built a little stage in it out of old boards. There is a

curtain the colour of flowerpots, a painted cloth at the back, and just

enough room on the boards for everybody at once.

This year, the stage is yours.

Everybody arrives. Nobody has played a note yet.

CURTAIN UP

You are going to sing. You are going to bang, shake, tap and

scrape. You are going to walk to a beat, make a journey out of

sound, pretend to be somebody else, and stand up in front of

people and show them what you made.

## Here is the one big idea of the whole book:

A sound is something you make on purpose.

## Not a noise that just happens. A sound that you choose. Loud or

soft. High or low. Fast or slow. Long or short. And you choose

when it starts, and when it stops.

HOW TO USE THIS BOOK

The same four things, every time

Every single thing you do in this book goes round the same little circle.

When you get stuck, come back here.

1
**Listen. Before you make any sound at all, hear it. Hear it outside you, or**
hear it in your head.

2
**Try. Have a go. It does not have to be right. The first go almost**
never is, and that is completely fine.

3
**Choose. Now pick. Louder or softer? Faster or slower? This one**
or that one? This is the important step, and it is the one people
forget.

4
**Show. Give it to somebody. A partner, the class, the whole**
school. A sound nobody hears is only half finished.

GETTING SET UP

Making a stage in your room

You do not need a real theatre. You need a space, and everybody

facing the same way.

Badger takes the rope. Bramble lays the instruments out in a row. Hattie sweeps the boards.

1
**Clear a space. Push the tables back. Leave a patch of floor big enough**
for four people to stand in a line.

2
**Mark the front. A line of tape on the floor is your edge of the stage.**
Do not stand behind it when it is your turn.

3
**Set out the instruments. In a row, along one side, all facing the**
same way. Then everyone can see what there is.

4
**Agree the two signals. One from your teacher that means**
**start, and one that means stop. A hand going up is the**
easiest stop signal there is.

✎FOR TEACHERS

A taped line and two agreed hand signals do more for a Year 1 music lesson than
any amount of equipment. Establish both before the first instrument comes out of
the cupboard.

GETTING SET UP

Four rules of the Hedgerow Theatre

Everybody who steps on these boards agrees to these. They are short

on purpose.

1
**When somebody is playing, everybody else is listening. That is the**
whole deal. You get listened to because you listen.

2
**An instrument is not a toy, and it is not a hammer. Play it the way**
it wants to be played, and it will still work next year.

3
**Silence is part of the music. The quiet before you start and the**
quiet after you finish both belong to the piece.

4
**Nobody laughs at a wrong note. Everybody plays wrong**
notes. The only people who do not are the people who never
try.

☞
**Safe ears. Loud is fun for a moment and tiring for an hour. If a sound**
makes you want to move away from it, that is your ears being sensible.
Listen to them.

## ♪PIP'S TRUE THING

Your ears can hear about 20 wobbles a second at the lowest and
20,000 a second at the highest. Anything slower or faster is silent to
you.

## ♫LINE BY LINE, AND WAIT

This is how everybody learns a song, including grown-up singers.

## One person sings one line. Then they stop and wait. Then

everybody sings that same line back. Then the next line. Then both

lines joined together. Then the next one.

The waiting is the important part. You cannot copy something

while it is still going on.

## ♫ACTIONS HOLD THE WORDS FOR YOU

Do the same action every time you sing a line, and your body starts

to remember the words for you. When you forget a word, do the

action and it very often comes back.

## ♫CALL AND ANSWER

## One person calls. Everybody answers. It is the oldest way of

singing together there is, and sailors, soldiers and farm workers all

used it, because you can sing it while your hands are busy.

The important bit is the gap. The answer starts exactly where the

call stopped: no sooner, and no later.

## ♫TAKING TURNS IS A MUSICAL SKILL

Waiting quietly for your turn does not feel like music, but it is. The

silence you leave is what makes the other person's line sound like a

line. Fill the gap, and you have wrecked both.

## ♫A SOUNDSCAPE IS A PICTURE MADE OF SOUND

No tune, no words. Just sounds chosen so carefully that somebody

with their eyes shut can tell where they are.

The choosing is the whole job. A shaker turned slowly is rain.

Turned fast it is hail. A drum tapped with fingertips is footsteps;

hit hard it is thunder. Same instruments, different choices.

## ♫GIVE IT A BEGINNING, A MIDDLE AND AN END

The commonest mistake is everybody playing everything all the

time, which sounds like a cupboard falling over.

## So: start with one sound. Add the others one at a time. Let it get to

its loudest in the middle. Then take the sounds away again, one by

one, until there is only one left. Then nothing.

✎FOR TEACHERS

Unit 3 is the notation unit. Stick notation and the ta / te-te syllables arrive here,
deliberately after a whole unit of chanting them in Topic 2.3, so the symbols
describe something pupils can already do.

The pulse and rhythm distinction in Topic 3.2 is the single most transferable idea in
the book. Pupils who can pat a steady pulse while saying a different rhythm have
the foundation for all Key Stage 1 music. It usually takes several short sessions
rather than one long one.

Every rhythm printed in this book is checked arithmetically before printing: each
bar is proved to fill its time signature, and the syllables and the stick notation are
generated from the same source, so they cannot disagree.

## ♫FOUR PARTS THAT ALWAYS WORK

## The pulse. One steady sound on every step. Safe, and it holds

## ♫REHEARSING MEANS FIXING THE JOINS

Playing the whole thing through from the beginning again and

again is the slowest way to get better, because you spend most of

the time on the bits that already work.

Find the place it goes wrong. Practise the four beats before it and

## ♫IF SOMEBODY GOES WRONG, KEEP GOING

In a rehearsal you stop and fix it. In a performance you do not: you

keep going, and almost nobody in the audience notices.

The only thing that turns a small mistake into a big one is

everybody stopping to look at it.

Songs we sing but do not print

This book prints a song's words only when those words are out of

copyright. The songs below are ones a Year 1 class may well sing, and

several are named in the units, but their words still belong to the

people who wrote them or to their families. So this book names them

and prints nothing.

That is a deliberate editorial position rather than an oversight. It also

turns out to be a good lesson: pupils in Topic 4.2 find the fairness

argument easy to follow, and it is worth making rather than glossing

over.
