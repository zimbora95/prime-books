## Combining different printing methods

**◧ Open the door**

What happens if the second layer is darker than the first?

One method gives you one kind of mark. Two methods on one sheet give you a
conversation: a soft mono-printed sky behind a crisp stamped bird, or a rough
texture under a clean edge. The skill in this topic is not making more marks. It
**is deciding the order.**

**▸ Here is how it works**

**Combine on purpose:**

Layer order is a decision you can explain. Pale wash first, sharp shape second
puts the sharp shape in front. Swap them and the sharp shape sinks behind a
veil. Neither is wrong; only one of them is what you meant.

**A pattern does not have to repeat exactly. Change the spacing, the pressure**

**Quiet areas do work**

**◧ Open the door**

**◧ Open the door**

How can one continuous wire look soft in one place and sharp in another?

**▸ Here is how it works**

**Density is your tone**

In a drawing you make an area darker by pressing harder. In wire you make it
darker by packing the loops closer, because a dense tangle stops the light and
an open loop lets it through. A wire sculpture has lights and darks exactly as a
drawing does, and you control them with spacing.

One of my pieces stands on its own.

**Look at a maker: Alexander Calder**

Calder moved to Paris in 1926 and began drawing with wire instead of a pencil.
His wire portraits are single continuous lines bent in the air: no shading, no
surface, just an outline that happens to be standing up. Hold one to a wall and
its shadow is a drawing.

Between 1926 and 1931 he built Cirque Calder, a miniature circus of small
figures made from wire, fabric and wood, with acrobats, a lion tamer and a
trapeze. He was not only the maker. He performed it himself, moving the
figures by hand in front of an audience, and the performances made his name
in Paris.

## Studio words

**WORD**
**WHAT IT MEANS**

**armature**
an inner support that helps a sculpture stand

**block-in**
the first large areas of colour, laid before any detail

**caricature**
a portrait with one feature deliberately pushed further than life

**collagraph**
a print from a plate built up with glued textures

**cool colours**
blues, cool greens and violets, which tend to sit back

**depth**
the sense of near and far on a flat surface

**dry brush**
little water on the brush, so the bristles drag and skip

**edition**
a set of prints pulled from the same plate, usually numbered

**en plein air**
painting outdoors, in front of the subject

**free-standing**
a sculpture that stands on its own base and can be walked around

**horizon line**
the eye-level line where sky meets ground in a drawing

**kento**
the registration marks cut into a Japanese woodblock so each
colour lands in place

**line of action**
the single curved line through a figure that carries the pose

**mono-print**
a one-off print; each pull is unique

**one-point**
**perspective**

depth lines meet at a single vanishing point

**plate**
the surface a printer inks, before the paper meets it

**proportion**
how the sizes of parts relate to each other

**pull**
one press of paper onto an inked plate

**registration**
getting a second layer to land exactly where you planned

**relief print**
a print from the raised parts of a plate or block
