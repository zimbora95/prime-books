Exploring printing techniques

◧ Open the door
If you could stamp one mark forty times before lunch, what mark would earn that
many presses, and who would it be for?

▸ Here is how it works
Printing lifts ink from a prepared surface onto paper. You prepare the surface
once, then repeat the press. That is the whole idea, and it is why printing is used
whenever many copies must match.

Each one has a trap. Thin lines on a relief block fill with ink and disappear. A
wobbly found object slides and smudges. A monoprint cannot be repeated at all.

In a relief print the ink sits on the parts that stand up, so the lines you press down
stay the colour of the paper. Beginners often expect the opposite, then wonder
why their drawing came out inside out.

Monoprint starts with mono, which means one. You roll ink onto a tray, draw into
it with a cotton bud or the wrong end of a brush, then lay paper on top. It gives
one print only, because drawing into the ink destroys the picture as it prints it.

Picture 1.1 Found objects and the marks they leave: cork, leaf, bottle lid, corrugated card and half a potato. Look for the
ribbed stripes from the card and the veins the leaf prints.

U N I T 1 - TO P IC 1 . 2

Using colour in printing

◧ Open the door

A poster must warn people about a wet floor in a busy corridor. Pale yellow on
cream, or deep blue on white? Say why.

▸ Here is how it works

Colour in a print is chosen, not left to chance.

Weak contrast makes a pretty print that nobody can read from three metres away.
Strong contrast lets one shape carry the length of a hall.

Picture 1.3 The sun stamp printed in two colours: the pale layer, then the dark layer, then both together. Look at the small
cross in the corner of every panel, the mark that tells you the second layer landed in the right place.

One colour is strong, quick and clear. Most badges and logos live on one colour.

Two colours need a plan: print the lighter colour first, let it dry, then print the
darker one on top.

Contrast is the difference between your ink and your paper, and it decides how
far away your print can still be read.

Registration means lining the second layer up with the first, by matching a
small cross mark outside the picture.

▸ Here is how it works

A final print is the best press of a planned design, on chosen paper, dried flat and
labelled. A set of matching final prints is called an edition, and printers number
them so nobody has to guess which is which.

Run the checklist before the final press.

Then press, peel, and hang it up. Write your name, the date and the job on the
back of every print, in pencil, once it is dry.

Picture 1.7 Prints from one block pegged up to dry, with a blank label slip clipped beside each one. Look at how alike they
are, because that likeness is what makes them an edition instead of a pile of separate tries.

Experimenting with brush techniques

◧ Open the door
Which brush would you use for a wide evening sky, and which for a thin fence

▸ Here is how it works

The brush is not one tool. It is five, and the difference is in how much water you
carry and how you land the stroke.

↺
D O YO U R E M E M B E R ?

In Unit 1 you chose a colour on purpose, so a print would carry across a
room. Painting asks the same question and then a harder one: not only
which colour, but how wet, how thick, and with which brush.

watercolour or ready-mix paint in red, yellow and blue, plus white

a flat brush and a round brush, and one old brush for dry work

two water pots, one for washing out and one kept clean

thick paper that will not cockle, plus scrap paper for tests

a rag or kitchen paper, a palette or an old white plate, an apron

one real object to paint from: fruit, a jug, a plant, a shell

✦
WA R M U P YO U R H A N D S

Load a brush with clean water and paint a wet stripe. Now touch one dot of
colour into the wet stripe and watch it travel by itself. That travelling is what
a wash does, and you steer it by how wet the paper is.

darker. Painters learn their own materials by keeping a test strip beside the
work and comparing it after it dries, instead of trusting the wet colour.

Primary colours are red, yellow and blue. You cannot mix them from anything
else.

Secondary colours come from two primaries: orange, green and purple.

A tint is a colour with white added, or with more water in watercolour.

A shade is a colour with a little of its opposite added, which is how you get a
rich grey instead of a dead one.

Warm colours (red, orange, yellow) feel busy and close. Cool colours (blue,
green, violet) feel calm and far away.

In a relief print the ink sits on the raised parts, so any line you press down stays
the colour of the paper.

A monoprint cannot be repeated, because drawing into the ink destroys the
picture in the act of printing it. Mono means one.

A found-object print takes the surface of a real thing, so corrugated card gives
ribbed stripes and a leaf gives its veins.

How this book is built

Your turn: the darker of the two colours almost always reads first from across
the room, because it differs more from the paper.

The second colour is lined up by matching the small registration cross, and the
lighter colour is printed first so the darker one can sit on top of it.

If the one-sentence job needs the word "and", the print is being asked to do
two jobs and should probably become two prints.

A passer-by who is walking needs one bold shape with plenty of space around
it. A tag held in the hand can carry finer detail, because the viewer is close.

A strong plan can say who looks at the print, and from how far away, before any
block is cut.

An edition is the set of prints that matched, not every print that was pulled.
Two rejects out of six pulls is ordinary and honest.

A pale print is not fixed by pressing harder, because pressing harder squashes
the block and spreads the shape. Re-ink thinly and press evenly instead.

The back of every final print carries the name, the date and the job, in pencil,
written after the print is dry.
