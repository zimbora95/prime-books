Come out to the long field

You already know how to run, jump, land, throw, catch and travel. You have been doing
all of it since Year 1, and by now you do most of it without thinking.

This year we are going to make you think about it, once, on purpose, and then let you
stop thinking about it again.

Here is the idea the whole book turns on. A movement is not one thing. A throw is a
stand, then a reach, then a release, then a finish. A jump is a run up, then a take off, then
a flight, then a landing. Every one of those parts can be practised on its own, and every
place where two parts meet is a join.

Most pupils in Year 5 are already reasonably good at the parts. Almost nobody is good
at the joins. That is where this book spends its time.

The long field before anybody arrives. Mown grass, one strip pegged out in white rope, the pavilion waiting with its three
steps, the stone wall and the orchard beyond. Look at the rope: nearly everything in this book happens because somebody
decided where a line should go.

What is different about Year 5

In Year 3 the useful answer was I can jump. This year it sounds more like this:

I can land my jump without a wobble, I can go straight from the landing into a turn
without stopping, I can do the whole thing slowly if you ask me to, and if somebody

About this book

This student book is for Year 5 Physical Education at Prime School, written for pupils
of about nine to ten. It sits after Years 1 to 4: those years taught you to move. This year
teaches you to look at a movement, refine one part of it, and then use that control in
games, in invented phrases, in a team job, and in looking after your own body.

You will practise technique (the parts and the joins), coordination, agility, speed and
endurance, and you will learn simple fitness language you can use on the field. You will
also practise teamwork, communication, decision-making, leadership, personal
responsibility and fair play. The book stays a PE book: it is not a specialist sports
manual and it is not a health textbook.

U N I T 1 - T O P I C 1 . 1

Practise that changes a skill

**○**
COME OUT TO THE FIELD

Think of one thing your body does well. Catching a ball. Skipping. Running round
a bend without slowing down.

Now the harder question. Which part of it are you good at?

Almost nobody can answer that, because a movement you can already do feels
like one single thing. It is not. Every movement you have is made of parts, and you
got good at it without ever seeing them.

On this ground you are going to see them.

▣
WHAT YOU NEED ON THE FIELD

One ball each, a wall you are allowed to throw at, five cones, a stopwatch, a notebook and a pencil.
Work in fours.

✦
WARM UP

Jog anywhere in the space for ninety seconds, changing direction every time you
meet somebody, without touching anybody. Then swing each arm forward five
times and backward five times, and rise onto your toes ten times.

Now stand still. You are warm, your shoulders are loose and your ankles are
awake. Cold practice is wasted practice.

▸ Learn

Doing a movement thirty times is not practising it. It is repeating it.

Practice means repeating a movement with one part you are trying to change. Without
that part, you are keeping fit, which is fine, but nothing gets better.

Three things make a practice work, and if any one is missing the other two are wasted.

◆

Pip is throwing a ball at a wall. He throws it thirty times, which takes him about four

Pip says that is the same as thirty throws.

It is not, and the difference between those two sentences is what this whole

parts got any attention and none of them changed.

DID YOU KNOW?

The NHS recommends that children and young people aged 5 to 18 "aim for an
average of at least 60 minutes of moderate or vigorous intensity physical activity
a day across the week".

Read the next part of the same guidance, though, because it is the part that
belongs in this unit. It asks for "a variety of types and intensities of physical
activity across the week to develop movement skills, muscles and bones".

Not just more minutes. A variety of them. Which is precisely what topic 1.3 is
about.

**→**
GO A LITTLE FURTHER

Try the same three step method on a skill nobody has taken apart for you: a chest
pass, an underarm serve, or a standing jump.

Write your own phase strip for it first, on paper, before you move. Four boxes and
three joins. If you cannot name the parts, you cannot practise them, and finding
that out is useful in itself.

▣
WHAT YOU NEED ON THE FIELD

One low bench, two mats, six cones, a stopwatch and a pencil. Work in fours. An adult sets out the
bench and the mats.

✦

Travel across the space and back four times, using a different way each time:
running, side steps, long slow strides, and skipping. Then stand on one foot for
ten seconds, and the other foot for ten seconds.

The assumptions this unit makes

A pupil cannot practise a whole skill. Every task therefore asks for one phase or
one join, chosen and named before anybody moves. If a class is choosing targets like
"throw better", the unit has not started yet.

The joins carry the objective. Objective 1.2 is assessed on the joins and not on the
quality of the individual skills, which is why the watcher counts stops rather than
awarding marks.

Variety is the weak point at this age, not control. Most Year 5 classes contain
several pupils with exactly one setting on every dial. Topic 1.3 exists for them, and
the cone method in Try it the other way is the reliable route in.

The Caller is not a game manager. It is an assessment role. Objective 1.4 asks for
adaptation to changing contexts, and a Caller is how that context changes on
purpose, in front of you, so you can see who adapts and who stops.

Assessment without a written test

The scheme lists a test in each of the three terms. This unit offers three pieces of
evidence that do not need one:

Nothing in this unit ranks one pupil against another, and no task requires a particular
body in order to succeed. Distances, heights and timings are adapted freely: the
objective is control, not size or speed.

Safety

Every Stay safe panel is an instruction rather than advice, and the panels are the only
magenta furniture in the book so that they cannot be mistaken for teaching. The unit
assumes a teacher present, a space checked before the lesson, and all apparatus set
out and tested by an adult. Topic 1.2 involves a bench, mats and possibly a roll, and
asks for adult supervision of every attempt. The track meet runs four stations at once
and asks for the whole ground to be walked before it starts.

The three calls a Caller may never make are named on the page: nothing that stops a
pupil seeing where they are going, nothing changed twice at once, and nothing called
quietly.

What a teacher will want to change

The tally tables in 1.1 and the timing tables in 1.2 and 1.3, which are a pupil
measured against their own earlier attempt.
