1.1 · The communication equation

Unit 1 · 1.1 · The communication equation

1.1 · The communication equation

DO YOU REMEMBER?
What is one message you sent today without using spoken words?

BOOTS ON!
In pairs, say "We need to talk" three ways: calm, worried, angry. Keep the words
identical. Note what changed.

LET US FIND OUT

Think of communication as an equation with four parts that must balance:

words + tone + body language + listening

If one part is missing or fights the others, the message wobbles. Clear words with a
sharp tone can feel like an attack. A kind face with vague words can leave people
guessing. Listening is not a free extra: without it, the loop never closes.

In a Lisbon classroom, "Can you help?" can mean "I am stuck" or "Do this for me",
depending on posture and voice. Year 6 communicators pause and check: "Did I hear
you right?" That check is part of the equation, not a delay.

1.2 · Bridging the language gap

Unit 1 · 1.2 · Bridging the language gap

1.2 · Bridging the language gap

DO YOU REMEMBER?
Have you ever tried to understand someone who spoke another language?

BOOTS ON!
Sixty seconds: invent four silent signs for water, toilet, help and thank you. Test them on
a partner. Adjust if they fail.

LET US FIND OUT

When people do not share a language, they build bridges: gestures, drawings,
simple shared words, translation tools (with adult care), and human interpreters.

Portugal is a strong place to notice bridges. Classmates may speak Portuguese at
home, or English, or languages from Portuguese-speaking countries overseas, or
something entirely new. A bridge is not a test of who is "clever". It is a fair tool
so both sides can be heard.

Shouting the same sentence louder is not a bridge. Slowing down, pointing to a
picture, and checking understanding are.

1.3 · Cultural codes

Unit 1 · 1.3 · Cultural codes

1.3 · Cultural codes

DO YOU REMEMBER?
Does everyone in your family greet people the same way?

BOOTS ON!
Stand up. Show a greeting you know: wave, handshake mime, bow of the head. No
touching if you prefer not to. Notice two differences in the room.

LET US FIND OUT

A cultural code is an unwritten habit about how people speak and behave:
how close to stand, how direct to be, whether interrupting is rude, how long to wait
before answering, whether eye contact is expected.

Codes are not "right" or "wrong" like a maths sum. They are patterns people learn.
In some homes, children speak only when invited. In others, lively overlap is normal.
In Portugal you may kiss cheeks in family settings; in other places a small bow or
handshake is preferred.

The Year 6 skill is notice, ask, respect. Mocking a style is not curiosity. Asking
"How do people usually greet in your family?" is.

1.4 · Respectful messages online

Unit 1 · 1.4 · Respectful messages online

1.4 · Respectful messages online

DO YOU REMEMBER?
What is the difference between a private chat and a public post?

BOOTS ON!
Write a three-word message that could be read two ways (for example "Sure, fine.").
Share how tone might be misread.

LET US FIND OUT

Online communication is still communication, but it has extra risks:
no face, fast send, and a message that can be forwarded beyond the people you chose.

Digital citizenship here means: write as if the person is in the room, check before
you send, never share a classmate's private photo or address, and tell a trusted
adult if something feels wrong.

Emojis can help tone, but they do not fix unkind words. Caps lock can feel like shouting.
A delayed reply is allowed. Silence is not always agreement. Screenshots travel.

1.5 · The art of persuasion

Unit 1 · 1.5 · The art of persuasion

1.5 · The art of persuasion

DO YOU REMEMBER?
When did someone convince you with a good reason, not by shouting?

BOOTS ON!
In forty seconds, persuade a partner to try a healthy snack using only two reasons and
one fact. No nagging.

LET US FIND OUT

Persuasion means inviting someone to agree using reasons, stories and evidence.
It is different from pressure (threats, shame, endless nagging) and from tricks
(false claims, hiding the downside).

Global ideas travel by persuasion too: climate campaigns, charity posters, sports
adverts, assembly talks. Year 6 pupils can share ideas in a class assembly, a poster,
or a short video script, but they still need facts they can check.

Fair persuasion asks three questions: What is my claim? What is my evidence? Who might
disagree, and why might they be right?

1.6 · Active listening

Unit 1 · 1.6 · Active listening

1.6 · Active listening

DO YOU REMEMBER?
What does your body do when you are really listening?

BOOTS ON!
Partner A speaks for thirty seconds about a weekend plan. Partner B must say it back in
their own words, then ask one open question. Swap.

LET US FIND OUT

Active listening means listening to understand, not only to reply.
Tools: calm body, no interrupting, say-it-back, and open questions
(How... Why... What happened next...).

In class discussion, active listening is how perspectives get a fair turn. In global
topics, it is how we avoid jumping to stereotypes about places we have never visited.

Listening is a skill. It can be practised like a sport. Year 6 listeners also notice
when they are planning their answer instead of hearing the person.

2.1 · Purpose of laws

DO YOU REMEMBER?
What is one rule that keeps people safe at school?

BOOTS ON!
In groups of three, invent a tiny island with eight people. Write three laws you would

LET US FIND OUT

Laws are formal rules made by a community or country so people can live
together with safety, fairness and predictability. Traffic laws, food safety
laws and school policies are different layers of the same idea: shared order.

Laws are not perfect. They can be improved when evidence shows harm. That is why
parliaments debate and update them. In Portugal, the national parliament is the
Assembly of the Republic. For Year 6, the key is: laws are for people,
including people who will never meet each other, and including children.

2.2 · Rights and responsibilities

Unit 2 · 2.2 · Rights and responsibilities

2.2 · Rights and responsibilities

DO YOU REMEMBER?
What is one right you have at school, and one duty that goes with it?

BOOTS ON!
Complete the pair: "I have the right to be heard. My responsibility is..." Share once with a
partner.

LET US FIND OUT

A right is something you are entitled to in a fair community: safety, learning,
respect. A responsibility (also called a duty) is what you do to protect other
people's rights.

Rights without responsibilities can become selfish. Responsibilities without rights
can become unfair control. Year 6 thinkers hold both together.

Example: you have a right to speak in a discussion. You also have a responsibility
not to shout over someone else. You have a right to a safe playground. You also have
a responsibility not to leave litter that could hurt others.

Prime School Press · www.primeschool.pt

**TEACHER NOTES**
