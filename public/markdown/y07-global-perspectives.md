## How investigators work

1. Ask a clear question.

2. Gather sources (not only one website).

3. Note fact vs opinion.

4. Compare perspectives.

5. Make a reasoned conclusion.

6. Reflect: what would you improve?

## Outputs you may create

Posters · presentations · short reports · surveys · designs ·
debates · podcasts (with permission)

## Safety and respect

●Be kind when discussing sensitive issues.

●No private data in surveys without adult rules.

●Credit sources.

●Disagree with ideas, not with insults.

**Personal · Local · Global**

Most issues can be seen on three scales. Strong answers
move between them.

**Source checklist**

**Check**
**Question**

Author
Who made this?

Purpose
Why was it made?

Date
Is it recent enough?

Evidence
Are claims supported?

Perspective
Whose voice is missing?

**Worked mini-example**

Claim: “Everyone has the same access to the internet.”

Better evaluation: Internet access differs by country,
income and place. One class survey is not enough to
describe the whole world.

**Practice**

1. Write one global issue you care about.

2. Name one personal, one local and one global angle.

3. List two places you could find evidence (with adult help).

## Session 2 — Judging sources about the
## future

**Research**

With teacher guidance, collect three source types about
digital change (news summary, statistic chart, opinion
article).

**Evaluate**

Use the source checklist. Which is best for predicting
school futures? Why?

**Create**

Note-taking grid: author · purpose · main claim · trust level
(1–5) · reason.

**Challenge output**

based on digital trends.

Include: problem, idea, who benefits, who might be left
out, two success criteria, one risk.

**Create**

actions).
