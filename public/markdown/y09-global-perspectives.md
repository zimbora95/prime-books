**Case file**
## The pump on Broad
## Street

London, August 1854. In Soho, a crowded part of the city,
people start to fall ill with cholera. It strikes fast. Within ten
days, more than 500 people in the area have died.

Most doctors at the time believe cholera spreads through ‘bad air’ from
rubbish and sewage. A local doctor, John Snow, has a different idea. He
**thinks something in the water is to blame.**

Snow does not just guess. He collects evidence. He gets the official list of
who has died and where they lived. He knocks on doors and asks families a
simple question: where did you get your water?

The answers keep pointing to one place: a public water pump on Broad
Street.

On 7 September, Snow presents his evidence to the local council. They are
not fully convinced, but they agree to remove the pump’s handle so nobody
can use it. The outbreak, already slowing, comes to an end. In total, 616
people die.

Later, a local priest called Henry Whitehead sets out to prove Snow wrong.
Instead, his own careful research finds the likely first case: a baby at 40
Broad Street. Water used to wash the baby’s nappies had been emptied into
a cesspit just a few steps from the well. Whitehead changes his mind, and
the two men work together.

Snow and Whitehead helped to change how the world thinks about disease.
**Snow is often called a founder of epidemiology.**

The pump without its handle. A copy of the pump

stands in the street today.

**cholera**

A disease caused by bacteria that

spreads through dirty water and

food. It causes severe diarrhoea

and can kill quickly if untreated.

**epidemiology**

The study of how diseases spread

through groups of people, and

how to control them.

**Think and note**
Snow changed people's minds with evidence. What evidence would change your mind about something you believe?

## 3 The invisible world

Micro- means very small. Macro- means very large.
Microorganisms, or microbes, are living things too small to see
without a microscope.

Microbes are everywhere: in soil, in the sea, in the air and inside you. Your
body is home to trillions of bacteria, and most of them are harmless or
helpful. They help you digest food and keep harmful microbes out.

Only a tiny fraction of microbes cause disease. Scientists estimate that
fewer than 1 in 100 kinds of microbe are a danger to humans. The ones that
**are dangerous are called pathogens.**

**How small is small?**

Each picture zooms into the one before. 1 µm (one micrometre) is a thousandth of a
millimetre.

Most viruses are up to 100 times smaller than a typical bacterium. They cannot
reproduce on their own. They must get inside a living cell and use it to make copies of
themselves.

**microorganism**

A living thing too small to see

without a microscope. Also called

a microbe.

**pathogen**

A microorganism (or other agent)

that causes disease.

**infectious disease**

A disease caused by a pathogen

getting into the body. Many, but

not all, can spread between

people.

**Five kinds of pathogen**

**Viruses**
Tiny packages of genetic material that hijack living cells to copy themselves.

**Examples: flu, measles, COVID-19**

**Bacteria**
Single cells. Most are harmless; some release poisons or damage tissue.

**Examples: cholera, tuberculosis, some food poisoning**

**Fungi**
Yeasts and moulds. Most are harmless; a few infect skin, lungs or blood.

**Examples: athlete’s foot, ringworm, thrush**

**Parasites**
Living things that live on or in a host. Here: malaria parasites inside red blood cells.

**Examples: malaria, Guinea worm disease**

**Prions**
Not alive at all: proteins that fold into the wrong shape (blue) and make normal ones (pink) do the
same. Very rare.

**Examples: Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD)**

The germ is a bacterium (1). It lives in the gut of infected

## The worm that is almost
## gone

Guinea worm disease has no vaccine and no medicine. Yet it is
close to becoming only the second human disease ever to be
wiped out, after smallpox.

People catch it by drinking water from ponds that contains tiny water fleas
carrying worm larvae. About a year later, an adult worm, which can be longer
than your arm, slowly pushes out through a painful blister in the skin, often
on the leg or foot. People in pain often cool the blister in water. That
releases new larvae into the pond, and the cycle starts again.

**How do you stop a disease with no**
**cure?**

You break the chain, again and again, in thousands of villages:

None of this works without teams: village volunteers, health workers,
national governments, and organisations such as The Carter Center and the
World Health Organization.

**eradication**

Removing a disease from the

whole world, permanently.

**provisional**

Not yet final. Provisional figures

can change when more checks

are done.

**Not only humans. Guinea worm**
also infects animals, especially
dogs in Chad. This makes the last
steps to eradication much harder.

## 5 Ask better questions

A research question works like a lens. A good one brings one thing sharply into focus.

**Climb the question ladder**

**Topic**
**Malaria**

Not a question yet. Where would you even start?

**Closed**
**Is malaria a disease?**

Yes. Done. A dead end.

**Fact-finding**
**How many people get malaria each year?**

One number answers it. Useful, but not enough.

**Too big**
**How can we stop all diseases?**

Nobody could answer this in a whole lifetime.

**Researchable**
**How well do bed nets protect young children from**
**malaria in Nigeria?**

Focused, needs evidence and judgement, and someone needs the answer.

**Five tests for a strong research**
**question**

**1**
**Focused. It is about one problem, in one place or one group of people.**

**2**

## 4 Myths that travel

Some claims move from book to website to video, growing on the way. A careful researcher
follows them back to where they started.

**Case: How many words for snow?**

**1911**
An anthropologist, Franz Boas, notes that Inuit languages use several different word roots for snow, such as
snow on the ground and falling snow.

**Later**
Writers repeat the example. Each retelling makes the number bigger: dozens, then a hundred, then
‘hundreds of words for snow’.

**1986–**
**1991**

Linguists trace the claim back and show how it grew. Some call it a ‘hoax’. The story now spreads that the
claim is false.

**Today**
Researchers who work with Inuit communities point out that Inuit languages build long words from many
parts, and that people who live on ice do use many precise terms for snow and sea ice. Counting ‘words’ is
the hard part.

**What this case teaches**
**primary source**

First-hand evidence from the

time or from the people involved.

**secondary source**

A source that describes or

explains other sources.

**Think and note**
Note a claim you have heard repeated many times. Where did it start?

A claim repeated by many sources is not stronger if they all copied each other.

Correcting a myth can create a new myth, if the correction is also simplified.

Some questions depend on definitions. Ask: what exactly is being counted?

Listen to the people the claim is about. Inuit speakers are the best source on Inuit
languages.

## 5 Lost in translation?

Translation apps can turn a menu, a sign or a message into your language in seconds. How
far can you trust them?

Machine translation has improved enormously. For many common
languages it is fast, free and usually good enough to understand the main
idea of a text.

But meaning is not only in the words. Jokes, idioms, politeness and tone
depend on culture. Languages with fewer speakers often have much less
text online for translation tools to learn from, so results can be weaker. And
a tool cannot tell you when it is wrong.

Write a short sentence in your best language.

## 1 What is poverty?

Poverty is not only about money. It is about what people can and cannot do with their lives.

**Absolute poverty means not having enough for basic needs: food, clean**
water, shelter, health care. The international poverty line measures this.

**Relative poverty means having much less than most people around you, so**
that you cannot take part in normal life in your society. A family can be
relatively poor in a rich country even if they would not be counted as
extremely poor worldwide.

**Many organisations also measure poverty in several dimensions at once,**
such as health, education and living standards. A family might have some
income but no school nearby, no clean water and no electricity.

The first of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals is to end
poverty in all its forms everywhere. Goal 10 is to reduce inequality within
and between countries.

**absolute poverty**

Not having enough for basic

human needs.

**relative poverty**

Having much less than most

people in your society, so you

cannot take part fully.

**inequality**

Unfair or large differences

between people or groups, for

example in income, health or

opportunities.

## 3 Plan your research

Good research starts with knowing what you need to find out, and where you might find it.

**Break your question into smaller sub-questions. Each one should help you**
answer the main question. For each sub-question, decide whether you need
secondary sources, primary research, or both.

Example. Main question: How does phone use at night affect sleep for students in my city, and what would help?

**1. What do experts say about screens and sleep? (secondary: health organisations,**
research summaries)

**2. How do students in my school use phones at night? (primary: anonymous survey)**

**3. What do parents and teachers think? (primary: two short interviews)**

**4. What have other places tried? (secondary: news, school policies abroad)**

## Command words

The first word of a task tells you what kind of thinking to do. Many lost marks come
from describing when the task said explain, or explaining when it said evaluate.

**Word**
**What it asks you to do**
**Start like this**

**Identify**
Find or name something.
One reason is…

**Describe**
Say what something is like or what happens. No reasons
needed.

The chart shows that…

**Explain**
Give reasons why or how.
This happens because… This means that…

**Compare**
Show similarities and differences.
Both…, but… whereas…

**Analyse**
Break down information to find patterns, causes and
consequences.

One cause is…, which leads to…

**Evaluate**
Judge how strong, useful or reliable something is, with
reasons.

This source is credible because…,
however…

**Justify**
Give strong reasons for a decision or conclusion.
I chose this because the evidence shows…

**Suggest**
Put forward an idea or a possible answer.
One possible solution would be…

**Reflect**
Think about your learning, your work or your views, and
how they changed.

At first I thought…, but now…

## The strong paragraph

Most strong written answers are built from paragraphs with four parts. Learn them and use them everywhere.

## P
**Point**

Make one clear claim.

## E
**Evidence**

Support it with facts, data or
an example, with the source.

## E
**Explain**

Show how the evidence
proves the point.

## J
**Judge**

Say how strong it is, or what it
means for the question.

**A weaker paragraph**

Tourism is bad for cities. There are too many tourists
everywhere and it is annoying for people. Venice has
lots of tourists.

A claim with no source, a vague example and no explanation
or judgement.

**A stronger paragraph**

Tourism can push local residents out of popular
cities. In Venice, reports in 2022 said the historic
centre had fewer than 50,000 residents, as many
homes became holiday rentals. This suggests that
when renting to visitors earns more, homes are lost
for local families. However, this is one city, so it may
not apply everywhere; we would need data from
other cities to be sure.

Point, evidence with a source, explanation, then a careful
judgement.
