**Curriculum. This book supports the Cambridge Early Years framework for Physical Education and**
follows the Prime School scheme of work for Year 2, covering all twenty-nine learning objectives
across its six units: Moving Well, Understanding Movement, Moving Creatively, Taking Part, Taking
Responsibility and Healthy Bodies. Prime School is solely responsible for the interpretation of that
framework in these pages.

**How this year differs from the last. Year 1 and Year 2 share the same scheme of work, and that is**
deliberate: a movement skill is not learned once. What changes is what the skill is for. In Year 1 each
skill was practised on its own. In this book every skill is played inside a game that has a stated rule, a
partner or a team, and a choice for the pupil to make. Each unit therefore carries two features the
**Year 1 book did not: the rule of the game, printed so that pupils can read it for themselves, and what**
**would you do?, which asks for a decision and prints no answer.**

**Text, design and illustration. Written and designed by the Prime Books editorial studio. Original**
watercolour illustration produced for Prime School. All illustrations © Prime School 2026.

**Links and codes. The printed codes in this book lead to pages published by other organisations, which**
Prime School does not control. Each address is also printed in words beside its code so that no device
is needed to find it. Every address was checked before this edition went to press. Teachers should
preview any linked page before showing it to a class.

**Safety. The physical activities in this book have been written for use with pupils aged six to seven**
under the direct supervision of a teacher. Teachers should carry out their own risk assessment of the
space, the floor surface, the apparatus and the group before any activity, and should follow their
school's own safety policy. Pupils should be dressed for movement, should have water available, and
should never be asked to continue when they are in pain or distress. Prime School does not accept
responsibility for the way in which these activities are carried out.

WELCOME
Out to the court

The games hall, early on a bright morning, before anyone has arrived.

Behind the school there is a small court. Somebody has marked it out
with chalk lines: a long rectangle, a circle in the middle, and a short line
at each end. Beyond the court there is a hedge, and beyond the hedge
there are hills.

When it rains, there is the hall instead. The floor is wooden and shiny,
and it has lines and circles painted on it too.

Every one of those lines is there because somebody agreed what it means.

Six friends come out to that court. Last year they learned to run, to
jump, to throw and to catch. This year they are going to do all of that
inside a game, and a game has three things a lesson on its own does not:
a rule, at least one other person, and something for you to decide.

You will be asked to choose a lot in this book. Nobody has printed the
right answer, because most of the time there is more than one.

✎For teachers

Every unit follows the same sequence: an opener, a start page carrying the objectives,
then one teaching page and two practice pages per topic, then two closing pages. The
features are introduced on this spread and never change meaning afterwards.

Two features are new at Year 2 and appear on every topic. The rule of the game is
printed rather than only announced, so that pupils can settle a dispute by reading.
What would you do? poses a genuine choice and prints no answer, because objective
2.4 asks pupils to use tactics and a tactic that has been given to them is not one.

The self-assessment wording is fixed at confident, almost there and needs more work.
Colour words and the traffic-light metaphor are deliberately absent throughout: in
physical education they tell a child that their body is wrong.

Pupils do not need to read this book to use it. Every page is designed to be read aloud
by the teacher while the class moves.

Nobody in this book is good at everything. Sorrel is the fastest, and she

learning. Find the step you are on, then aim at the next one. That is all
improving is.

✎For teachers

The ladder above is deliberately not a mark scheme. Pupils at this age improve when
they can see the next step, and disengage when they are ranked against each other.
Name the step, never the child.

The third rung is worded differently from the Year 1 equivalent on purpose.
Performing a skill in isolation and performing it under the pressure of a rule and an
opponent are different attainments, and this book is about the second. Expect pupils
who were secure last year to drop a rung when the game arrives, and tell them that is
expected.

Objective 4.5 asks pupils to describe their own strengths and areas for improvement.
That is only possible once they have a shared vocabulary for what improvement looks
like, which this page supplies and every later unit reuses.

✎For teachers

This unit is the one that makes the rest of the book teachable. Pupils who can name
an action, a speed, a space and a relationship can be given feedback in Units 3 to 6;
pupils who cannot will hear every correction as praise or blame.

Topic 2.3 is worth slowing down for. Agreeing a criterion before watching is a habit,
not a lesson, and it is the habit that makes peer feedback useful rather than social.
Insist on the criterion being said aloud before the first go, every time, until they do it
unprompted.

Topic 2.4 carries objective 2.4 whole: rules AND tactics. The two are deliberately
taught as opposites in one lesson, because pupils this age routinely treat a tactic as a
rule they have been given. Expect to say that a choice is theirs and not the rule many
times over, and treat every defended choice as correct even when you would have
chosen otherwise.

✎For teachers

The instruction to be creative does not work at this age, and this unit deliberately
never gives it. Every creative task here is a CONSTRAINT: change one thing, find
three ways, answer this task, use no face. Constraints are what produce invention at
six; open invitations produce copying and freezing.

The four dials introduced in topic 3.1, how high, how fast, which way and how big, are
the spine of the whole unit and are reused in 3.4 to build a mood. If pupils cannot
name all four by the end of 3.1, do not move on: 3.4 becomes mime without them.

Topic 3.2's rule, find three ways before you choose, is worth enforcing even when it
feels slow. The first idea is almost always the class's shared idea and the third is
almost always the pupil's own.

Expect the no-face rule in 3.4 to be broken constantly and cheerfully. Reset it by
naming the dial you want instead of the feeling: ask for lower and slower rather than
for sadder.

Nothing in this unit is a movement skill, and all of it is assessable. The tally in topic

Topic 5.3 puts two beliefs in a character's mouth and then argues with them: that the

How this book is built

✎For teachers

This manual covers the twenty-nine objectives of the Prime School Physical Education
scheme of work for Year 2, across six units and twenty-four topics. The coverage
table earlier in this section prints every objective and the topics that claim it. That
table is generated from the same data the book is built from, so it cannot drift from
what the pages actually teach.

The Year 2 scheme of work is identical to the Year 1 scheme, and that is deliberate
rather than an oversight: a movement skill is not learned once. What changes is what
the skill is for. In Year 1 each skill was practised on its own. In this book every skill is
played inside a game that has a stated rule, a partner or a team, and a choice for the
pupil to make. Two features carry that difference and appear in every single topic:
THE RULE OF THE GAME, printed so pupils can read it themselves, and WHAT
WOULD YOU DO?, which poses a decision and deliberately prints no answer.

Expect pupils who were secure last year to look less secure for a lesson or two.
Performing a skill in isolation and performing it under the pressure of a rule and an
opponent are different attainments, and the skill ladder's top step is worded for the
second. That apparent dip is the year working, not a regression.

Every topic follows the same shape: a warm up, a short teaching passage, the rule of
the game, a modelled attempt that ends in a stated finding, twelve practice items, a
decision, a safety panel, and a how do you know question. Nothing in that sequence is
decoration. The modelled attempt in particular is there because pupils this age copy a
demonstrated process far more readily than they follow an instruction.
