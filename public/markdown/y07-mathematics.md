UNIT 1 · TOPIC 1.1

Adding and subtracting integers

Learn

Think of the number line as a road. Adding a positive number moves you to the right; subtracting a positive number

moves you to the left. The same is true for negatives, with one twist that surprises everyone at first: subtracting a
negative number moves you to the right.

Why? Because subtraction is the inverse operation of addition. To subtract a number, you add its inverse. The

inverse of 3 is −3, and the inverse of −8 is 8. So the calculation 2 − (−4) becomes 2 + 4, which is 6.

You can use rounding to estimate before you calculate, which is a good habit: it tells you whether your final answer is
even plausible.

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 .1

Work out:

a. −7 + 12 b. 5 + (−9) c. −4 − (−8)

a. −7 + 12  =  5     start at −7, move 12 right
b. 5 + (−9) = −4     start at 5, move 9 left
c. −4 − (−8) = −4 + 8 = 4   subtracting a negative adds its inverse

Why: In part c the two negatives do not cancel by magic: to subtract −8 we add its inverse, +8.

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 . 2

Estimate 198 + 347 by rounding each number to the nearest ten.

198 → 200     347 → 350
estimate: 200 + 350 = 550

Why: The exact answer is 545, which sits close to our estimate of 550, so we can be confident.

UNIT 1 · TOPIC 1.2

Multiplying and dividing integers

Learn

Multiplication began as repeated addition: 3 × 4 means 3 + 3 + 3 + 3. That picture still works when one factor is

negative. (−3) × 4 means (−3) + (−3) + (−3) + (−3), which is −12. So a negative times a positive is negative.

Division is the inverse of multiplication, so the same sign logic flows through: because 3 × 4 = 12, we know 12 ÷ 4 = 3;
and because (−3) × 4 = −12, we know −12 ÷ 4 = −3. Whenever exactly one of the two numbers is negative, the product

or quotient is negative. When both are negative, the result is positive, because the two negatives undo each other.

When brackets appear, work inside them first.

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 . 3

Work out:

a. 7 × (−4) b. (−9) × (−5) c. (−42) ÷ 6

a. 7 × (−4)  = −28   positive × negative = negative
b. (−9) × (−5) = 45  negative × negative = positive
c. (−42) ÷ 6 = −7    negative ÷ positive = negative

Why: Multiply or divide the absolute values first, then fix the sign: same signs give +, different signs give −.

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 . 4

Work out 5 × (2 + (−7)).

inside brackets: 2 + (−7) = −5
then: 5 × (−5) = −25

Why: Brackets first, always. The multiplication waits until the bracket is a single number.

UNIT 1 · TOPIC 1.4

Highest common factors

Learn

A factor of a number divides it exactly. The factors of 36 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36, and they come in pairs that
multiply to 36: 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 6 × 6. A common factor of two numbers is a factor of both, and the largest

of these is the highest common factor, or HCF.

The HCF has a beautiful use: it simplifies fractions as far as they can go. To reduce a fraction to its simplest form,

divide top and bottom by their HCF.

b. Hence simplify the fraction 24/60.

UNIT 1 · TOPIC 1.6

Square roots and cube roots

Learn

When you multiply a number by itself you get a square number: 5 × 5 = 25, so 25 is a square number, and we write 5²

= 25. The little 2 is an index, and it means "multiply this number by itself". Going the other way, the number that was
squared to give 25 is the square root of 25, written √25 = 5. Squaring and taking a square root are inverse operations.

Cubes work the same way with one more multiplication: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, so 64 is a cube number, written 4³ = 64. The

cube root undoes it: ∛64 = 4.

Most numbers are not perfect squares, but you can still trap their roots between two whole numbers.

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 . 8

Work out:

a. √144 b. √169 − √64 c. ∛216

a. 12 × 12 = 144,  so √144 = 12
b. √169 = 13 and √64 = 8,  so √169 − √64 = 13 − 8 = 5
c. 6 × 6 × 6 = 216,  so ∛216 = 6

Why: Every root is found by asking "what number, raised to this power, gives this result?"

∎
W O R K E D E X A M P L E 1 . 9

Between which two consecutive whole numbers does √50 lie?

7² = 49 and 8² = 64
49 < 50 < 64,  so 7 < √50 < 8

Why: Because 50 sits between two consecutive squares, its root sits between the corresponding consecutive
roots.

GLOSSARY

Unit 1

The mathematical words this unit has taught you, gathered in one place.

common factor: a number that is a factor of two or more numbers.

common multiple: a number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

consecutive: following one after another in order.

cube number: a number made by multiplying an integer by itself twice, such as 27 = 3³.

cube root: the number that cubes to give the value under the root, as in ∛27 = 3.

digit: any of the symbols 0 to 9 used to write numbers.

divisible: able to be divided exactly, with no remainder.

factor: a number that divides another exactly.

highest common factor (HCF): the largest common factor of two or more numbers.

index: the small raised number showing a power, as in 5².

integer: a positive or negative whole number, or zero.

inverse: the number that undoes another; the inverse of 4 is −4.

inverse operation: an operation that reverses another; subtraction is the inverse operation of addition.

lowest common multiple (LCM): the smallest common multiple of two or more numbers.

multiple: the result of multiplying a number by a positive integer.

<!-- page 28 -->

---
number line: a line on which numbers are placed in order.

product: the result of a multiplication.

quotient: the result of a division.

square number: a number made by multiplying an integer by itself, such as 36 = 6².

square root: the number that squares to give the value under the root, as in √36 = 6.

test for divisibility: a quick rule that tells you whether one number divides another without doing the division.
