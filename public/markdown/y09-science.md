Photosynthesis

Learn

Photosynthesis is the process that keeps almost every living thing alive. In it, plants use light energy to build

glucose from carbon dioxide and water. It is the only significant way that energy enters the living world.

Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs

light, mostly red and blue light, and reflects green, which is why leaves look green to you.

Notice the elegance of it: respiration, which you met in Year 8, is almost exactly the reverse. Photosynthesis stores

energy; respiration releases it.

## More about photosynthesis

When the sun is bright, leaves often build more sugar than they can spend. Rather than waste it, the plant banks
the extra, changing it into starch and tucking it away inside its chloroplasts and other cells. Later, on a dull day or
during the night, the starch is turned back into sugar and used. A simple starch test on a leaf tells you whether
photosynthesis has been happening there.

And because only some plant cells own chloroplasts, only those cells can photosynthesise. The glucose made by
photosynthesis has three main uses. It is used in respiration, releasing energy for growth. It is converted into
starch for storage. And it is turned into cellulose for cell walls, and into proteins for growth, when combined with
minerals from the soil.

Figure 1.1  A chloroplast, where light energy is captured. Photosynthesis happens inside the
chloroplasts, which are inside some of the cells in a leaf.

**Chloroplasts and chlorophyll**

Chloroplasts are small green organelles in plant cells. A single leaf cell may contain ﬁfty or more. Each has two
membranes and is ﬁlled with ﬂuid called the stroma. Suspended in it are stacks of ﬂattened discs called grana; each
disc is a thylakoid membrane, and this is where the chlorophyll sits. Chlorophyll is a pigment: it strongly absorbs red
and blue light and uses their energy for photosynthesis, but it cannot use green light, so green is reﬂected into your
eyes. That is why plants look green.

**A look inside a leaf**

Chloroplasts are mostly inside the cells in the middle layer of the leaf. Leaves are very thin, so it is easy for sunlight to reach these cells.
Chloroplasts also need lots of water, which comes to the leaf through the veins. Carbon dioxide diﬀuses into the leaf from the air through
tiny holes called stomata. In singular form, one hole is called a stoma. Oxygen also diﬀuses out through the air spaces after photosynthesis
and respiration. A leaf is so thin that, if you cut one across and place the cut edge under a microscope, all of these layers can be seen
clearly, as Figure 1.2 shows.

A leaf, with its veins bringing water to every cell. The red dashed line shows where to cut

## Factors limiting photosynthesis

Learn

A leaf is a solar panel with plumbing. Every feature has a purpose.

broad and flat
A large surface area to catch light.

thin
Gases diffuse only a short distance.

many chloroplasts near the top
Where the light is strongest.

Photosynthesis does not always run at full speed. A limiting factor is whatever is holding
it back at that moment: usually light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or
temperature. If you increase light intensity, the rate rises, then levels off, because
something else has become the limiting factor.

Temperature behaves differently: the rate rises, peaks, then falls sharply, because the
enzymes that drive photosynthesis are damaged, or denatured, by heat.

## Minerals and plant growth

**Learn**

**Photosynthesis builds sugar from gas and water, but sugar is not all a plant needs. To make proteins, a**

**plant must have nitrogen. To make chlorophyll, it must have magnesium. These raw materials arrive**

**not as food but as minerals: salts dissolved in the water that roots draw up from the soil.**

Roots are built for collecting. Fine hairs just behind the growing tip multiply the surface area, and each

**hair takes in water and dissolved minerals. Two minerals matter most. Nitrates supply nitrogen for**

growth: without them a plant is stunted and its leaves turn pale yellow, because it cannot build the

**proteins for new cells. Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll: a shortage shows as yellowing**

leaves, because without magnesium the green pigment cannot be made and photosynthesis slows.

**This is why farmers and gardeners add fertiliser. A fertiliser replaces the minerals that crops remove**

from the soil, so the next crop can grow as fast as the last. But more is not always better. Too much

fertiliser drains into streams and ponds, where it feeds algae instead, which then bloom, die and rot,

using up the oxygen that ﬁsh need.

The carbon cycle

Learn

Carbon atoms are never created or destroyed. They circulate endlessly between the air, living things, the oceans and

the rocks. This circulation is the carbon cycle.

Carbon goes OUT of the air by:

Carbon returns TO the air by:

For millions of years these processes roughly balanced. Some carbon, though, was locked away underground as
fossil fuels: coal, oil and natural gas, formed from organisms that died long ago and did not fully decompose. That
carbon was out of the cycle entirely.

When we burn fossil fuels, we return that ancient carbon to the air in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis
and the oceans can remove it. The cycle no longer balances, and carbon dioxide accumulates.

Climate change

Learn

Some gases in the atmosphere, called greenhouse gases, let sunlight through but absorb the infrared radiation the

Earth emits. This keeps the planet warm enough for life, and is called the greenhouse effect. It is entirely natural
and entirely necessary.

The problem is its strengthening. By adding carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere, human activity is

trapping more heat than before. This is enhanced global warming, and the resulting shifts in weather patterns are
climate change.

The evidence

The measurements are real, public and easy to check. At the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii, carbon dioxide has
been recorded continuously since 1958.

That is a rise of more than 50% above the pre-industrial value, and the rate of increase is itself increasing. Ice cores
show that for the previous 800 000 years, carbon dioxide never rose above about 300 ppm.

Consequences

Portugal is directly affected. Longer, hotter summers raise wildfire risk in the interior, and the Algarve faces

increasing pressure on its water supply.

Rising global mean temperature, and more frequent heatwaves.

Melting ice sheets and glaciers, and rising sea level.

Ocean acidification, as seawater absorbs carbon dioxide.

Shifting rainfall, bringing drought to some regions and floods to others.

## Atomic structure and the Periodic Table

**Everything around you is built from about one hundred diﬀerent elements. Each element is made of tiny**

particles called atoms. An atom is not the smallest thing there is: inside it are even smaller particles,

**called protons, neutrons and electrons.**

**Protons and neutrons sit together in the nucleus at the centre of the atom. Protons carry a positive**

charge and neutrons carry no charge at all. Electrons, which are negative, move around the nucleus in

shells. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges cancel.

**Two numbers describe any atom. The atomic number counts the protons; it ﬁxes the element's identity**

**and its place in the Periodic Table. The mass number counts protons plus neutrons. A lithium atom, for**

example, has atomic number 3 and mass number 7, so it has 3 protons, 3 electrons and 4 neutrons.

**The Periodic Table lists the elements in order of atomic number. Rows are called periods and columns**

are called groups. Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell,

which is why they behave in similar ways.

**Calculating protons, neutrons and electrons**

**The rules. The atomic number (proton number) is the number of protons. The mass number is protons +**
neutrons. In a neutral atom, electrons = protons. So:

protons = atomic number   ·   neutrons = mass number − atomic number   ·   electrons = atomic number
**Worked example: chlorine, atomic number 17, mass number 35**

**Protons**
= atomic number
= 17 protons

**Neutrons**
= mass number · atomic number = 35 · 17
= 18 neutrons

**Electrons**
= protons (neutral atom)
= 17 electrons

## Trends in groups within the Periodic Table

**Learn**

**A quick look along a group reveals patterns that chemists rely on. Group 1 contains lithium, sodium and**

potassium: soft metals that ﬂoat on water and react vigorously with it, producing hydrogen gas. As you

go down the group the metals become more reactive, because the outer electron is further from the

nucleus and more easily lost.

**The halogens in Group 7 show the opposite journey: ﬂuorine and chlorine are reactive gases, bromine**

is a liquid, and iodine is a dark solid. Reactivity falls as you descend, because the incoming electron is

further from the nucleus and less strongly attracted.

**The noble gases in Group 0 are the odd ones out. Their outer shells are full, so they neither lose nor**

gain electrons. That makes them almost completely unreactive, which is why they glow steadily in lamps

and protect hot ﬁlaments from burning.

## Materials of Groups 1, 2 and 7

**Group 1 · alkali metals. These are soft enough to cut with a knife, light enough to ﬂoat on water, and**

silvery when freshly cut, though the shine soon tarnishes in air. They are kept under oil, because both air

and moisture attack them. Lithium, sodium and potassium all melt below 200 °C, so a warm day leaves

them soft and sluggish. Their low density and eagerness to give away an outer electron make them

useful in batteries and in street lamps that glow orange.

**Group 2 · alkaline earth metals. Harder, denser and calmer than their Group 1 neighbours. Magnesium**

ribbon burns with a brilliant white light, once used in camera ﬂashes and still used in ﬁreworks; calcium

ﬁzzes gently in water and builds our bones and teeth along with carbon and oxygen; beryllium is light

and stiﬀ enough for specialist instruments. They hold their two outer electrons more tightly, so they need

more persuasion to react.

**Group 7 · halogens. Colourful, pungent non-metals. Fluorine is a pale yellow gas so reactive it attacks**

glass; chlorine is a greenish gas used in tiny amounts to kill bacteria in drinking water and swimming

pools; bromine is one of only two elements that are liquid at room temperature, a deep red-brown,

fuming liquid; iodine forms shiny grey crystals that vaporise into a violet gas. Their colours darken

steadily down the group, from pale yellow to nearly black.

**The metals of Groups 1 and 2: reactivity rises. A metal reacts by giving away its outer electrons. Down**

full shells in between block, or shield, the nucleus's pull. A distant, shielded electron is easily lost, and an

than their Group 1 neighbours.

its shell. Down the group, the incoming electron must land in a shell further from the nucleus, where the

attraction is weaker and more shielded. Fluorine's new electron drops into the second shell, close to a

strong pull, so ﬂuorine is the most reactive non-metal of all; iodine's must reach the ﬁfth shell, far out, so

iodine reacts slowly and gently. The same distance rule decides both groups, but in opposite directions.

## The noble gases: why they are noble

**A full shell changes everything**

**NEON · Group 0**
**SODIUM · Group 1**

**Why noble? The name came from chemistry's early days, when gases that refused to react with anything**

were called noble, aloof and superior, standing apart from the crowd like nobility. The reason is simple and

beautiful: their outer electron shells are completely full. Helium holds 2, neon 8, argon 8. An atom reacts

only to complete its outer shell, by giving electrons away or pulling them in. A noble gas needs to do

neither. It is born satisﬁed, so it has nothing to gain from reacting, and nothing to spare.

**Proof by extremes. Fluorine, the hungriest element of all, attacks almost every metal, bursts water into**

ﬂame, even etches glass. Yet even ﬂuorine cannot persuade neon or argon to react under normal

conditions. When even the ﬁercest reagent gives up, the refusal is absolute.

**Useful because unreactive. Their indiﬀerence is exactly what makes them useful. Argon ﬁlls light bulbs**

and welding shields, because it will not burn the ﬁlament or react with hot metal. Helium lifts party balloons

and airships, and its full, light atoms escape gravity's grip only slowly. Neon glows orange-red in signs

when excited by electricity, then settles back, unchanged.

## Why elements react to form compounds

**Learn**

Atoms are most stable when their outer shell of electrons is full. Atoms that lack a full outer shell react

**with other atoms to complete it, and the result is a compound: a substance in which two or more**

elements are chemically joined.

**There are two main ways to ﬁnish an outer shell. A metal atom may donate its outer electrons to a non-**

metal, leaving a positive ion attracted to the negative ions that receive them. Sodium chloride, common

**salt, is built this way from Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Or two non-metal atoms may share electrons, as in water**

**and carbon dioxide. Shared pairs of electrons are called covalent bonds.**

A compound is very diﬀerent from a mixture. In a mixture the ingredients keep their own properties and

can be separated by physical means. In a compound the elements are chemically bonded, have new

properties entirely, and can only be separated by a chemical reaction. Table salt bears no resemblance

to a soft grey metal and a poisonous green gas.

## Ionic and covalent bonds

**An ionic bond forms when electrons are transferred. A sodium atom has one lonely electron in its**

outer shell; a chlorine atom has seven and needs one more. Sodium hands its spare electron across, and
in that single exchange both atoms end up with full outer shells. The sodium atom, having lost a negative

electron, is left as a positive sodium ion, Na⁺; the chlorine atom, having gained one, becomes a negative
chloride ion, Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract, and that attraction, holding millions of ions together in a lattice,
is the ionic bond. Ionic bonds are strong, which is why salt crystals are hard and only melt at very high

temperatures.

**A covalent bond forms when electrons are shared. Two hydrogen atoms each hold a single electron,**

half a shell each. Instead of transferring, they overlap their shells and share the pair, so each atom enjoys
a full shell part of the time. That shared pair is the covalent bond, drawn as a dash, H—H. Covalent

bonding is the rule between non-metals: water, carbon dioxide and methane are all built from shared
electrons. The bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the molecules attract each other only weakly,

so simple molecular substances melt and boil at low temperatures.

**How to tell them apart. Metal plus non-metal usually means transfer, ions and an ionic bond; two non-**

metals usually means sharing, molecules and covalent bonds.

## Covalent molecules: oxygen, carbon dioxide and ammonia

Figure 2.3  Three covalent molecules, with their shared electron pairs circled.

**Oxygen (O₂) is simply two oxygen atoms sharing two pairs of electrons, a double bond. Each atom needed**

two more electrons to ﬁll its shell, and both are satisﬁed by the same two shared pairs. This is the molecule

you breathe; nothing else shares its electrons so generously.

**Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is a straight line of three atoms: oxygen, carbon, oxygen again, each pair joined by a**

double bond. Notice how one carbon atom completes its shell by sharing with two neighbours at once. Plants

take in this molecule and split it apart to build sugar.

**Ammonia (NH₃) is a pyramid: one nitrogen atom on top, three hydrogens around its base, each joined by a**

single shared pair. Nitrogen came to covalent bonding needing three electrons, so it makes three bonds.

That spare pair of electrons sitting alone on top of the nitrogen is what gives ammonia its sharp smell and its

use in cleaning products.

## More covalent molecules: chlorine, water and methane

Figure 2.4  Three more covalent molecules: chlorine, water and methane.

**Chlorine (Cl₂) is the simplest picture of covalent bonding: two identical atoms, each needing one more**

electron, sharing a single pair between them. Because both atoms pull equally, the pair sits exactly halfway.

This pale green gas is the same chlorine that puriﬁes swimming pools, and it is so eager to complete its shell

that it reacts with almost anything that can give an electron away.

**Water (H₂O) is not straight, but bent. Oxygen needed two more electrons, so it makes two bonds, one to**

each hydrogen, with two lone pairs left over sitting on top. Those lone pairs push the bonds closer together,

squeezing the angle to about 104°. That bent shape, with its uneven sharing, is why water dissolves salt,

climbs up plant stems and freezes into a lattice that ﬂoats.

**Methane (CH₄) is carbon's tidy masterpiece. Carbon needed four electrons, so it makes four identical bonds**

to four hydrogens, arranged as a three-pointed pyramid in three dimensions, called a tetrahedron. Each

bond points as far from the others as geometry allows. Methane is the main gas in natural gas, and the same

four-bonded carbon builds everything from petrol to proteins.

## Metallic bonding: the third kind

**A metal bond is a bond to the crowd. Ionic bonding transfers electrons from one atom to another;**

covalent bonding shares them between two. Metallic bonding does neither. Instead, every atom in the

metal donates its loose outer electrons to the whole structure at once. The atoms left behind become

positive ions, packed in regular rows, while the donated electrons drift freely between them, a wandering

swarm called a sea of electrons.

**The glue is attraction. What holds the metal together is the pull between the positive ions and the**

negative sea around them. The electrons belong to no single atom, so the bonding spreads through the

entire lump of metal in every direction, which is why metals are strong yet ﬂexible rather than brittle like

salt crystals.

**The sea explains everything. Because the free electrons drift wherever a push sends them, metals**

conduct electricity: connect a battery and the whole sea ﬂows. The packed ions let whole layers slide past

each other without breaking the bond, which is why metals bend, hammer ﬂat and draw into wires. And the

moving electrons carry energy swiftly from hot end to cold, which is why metals feel cold to the touch and

make such good pans.

## Simple and giant structures

**Learn**

**How the particles in a substance are arranged explains how it behaves. In simple molecular**

**substances such as water, carbon dioxide and methane, a few atoms are held tightly together in**

molecules, but the molecules attract each other only weakly. Little energy is needed to pull them apart,

so these substances melt and boil at low temperatures and many are gases or liquids at room

temperature.

**Metals are diﬀerent again. Their atoms pack in a giant metallic structure of positive ions in a sea of**

loose outer electrons. The electrons wander freely, which is why metals conduct electricity so well; the

packed ions let layers slide, which is why metals can be bent and hammered into shape.

**Ionic compounds such as sodium chloride form a giant ionic lattice, a repeating pattern of alternating**

positive and negative ions held by strong attraction. It takes great energy to break, so ionic compounds

have high melting points, and they conduct electricity only when dissolved or molten, when their ions are

free to move.

## Diamond and graphite: two giant covalent structures

**Diamond and graphite are both made of nothing but carbon atoms, yet they could not be more**

**diﬀerent. The secret is the bonding. In diamond, every carbon atom uses all four of its bonds, reaching**
out to four neighbours in a rigid three-dimensional network that repeats without end. Nothing in the

structure can move or slip, so diamond is the hardest natural material known, and its tightly held electrons
give it a sparkling, glassy brilliance.

**Graphite bonds diﬀerently. Each carbon atom joins only three neighbours, locking atoms into ﬂat sheets**

of hexagons, stacked layer upon layer. The fourth electron of every atom is left free to wander, which is
why graphite conducts electricity, alone among non-metals. The layers themselves attract each other only

feebly, so they glide apart at the lightest touch: that is what happens when a pencil slides across paper,
leaving a smear of layers behind.

**Same element, opposite characters. One is a transparent gem used to cut glass; the other is a black,**

slippery solid that lubricates locks and powers electrodes. Bonding, not the kind of atom, decides
everything.

## Heat and temperature

**Learn**

Heat and temperature are not the same thing, and confusing them is the most common mistake in this

**topic. Temperature tells you how fast the particles in a substance are moving on average. Heat is**

thermal energy on the move, ﬂowing always from a hotter place to a colder one.

A bathtub of lukewarm water holds far more thermal energy than a red-hot nail, because there are so

many more particles sharing the energy between them. A cup of tea at 80 °C will not warm a swimming

pool; a bath at 40 °C will warm you, because it has the bulk to keep giving.

Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius. Heat is measured in joules (J), like all

energy. Keep the two ideas separate in your mind: temperature is the reading on the dial, heat is the

ﬂow of energy down the gradient.

until both reach the same temperature and the readings stop changing. Heat has ﬂowed from hotter to
colder, and never the other way.

**Part 2: temperature is not energy. Compare two thermometer readings: a small cup of water at 80 °C and**
a bucket at 40 °C. Predict which could warm a cold room more. Test by touching the outside of each
container (carefully). The bucket holds far more thermal energy because it has so many more particles,
even though its temperature is lower.

**Make it fair. Use the same amount of water in Part 1 for every trial, keep the thermometer bulbs fully**
submerged but not touching the container, and read each thermometer at eye level.

**U N I T 3 · T O P I C 3.3**
## Conservation of energy

**Learn**

One of the grandest rules in science says that energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only

**transferred from one store to another. This is the conservation of energy.**

Energy is held in stores: kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic, thermal and others. A dropped

ball trades gravitational potential energy for kinetic energy as it falls, then, on landing, passes almost all

of it to the thermal store of the ground and the air. Nothing has vanished; it has simply spread out where

it is harder to use.

**Energy that spreads into the surroundings like this is called dissipated energy. It is not lost, but it is no**

longer concentrated enough to be useful. Every machine, every living thing and every power station

obeys the same rule, which is why engineers care so much about reducing unwanted transfers.

**U N I T 3 · T O P I C 3.4**
## Moving from hot to cold

**Learn**

Thermal energy always transfers from hotter places to colder places, never the other way round. Your

warm hands and a cold drink can only end up at one temperature: the point between their two starting

temperatures.

This one-way traﬃc explains everyday puzzles. When a window is opened on a cold day, thermal energy

ﬂows out of the room, and the room feels colder. A metal spoon in hot soup feels hot quickly because

metal conducts the energy towards your hand; a wooden spoon hardly warms at all, because wood is a

**poor conductor, a thermal insulator.**

**Transferring thermal energy is called heat dissipation when the energy spreads into stores where it**

was not wanted. The surroundings are always the ﬁnal destination.

**U N I T 3 · T O P I C 3.5**
## Ways of transferring thermal energy

**Learn**

**Thermal energy travels in three ways. Conduction is the passing of energy from particle to particle,**

**mainly in solids; metals conduct best because their free electrons carry energy swiftly. Convection**

happens in liquids and gases: a warm ﬂuid expands, becomes less dense, rises, and cooler ﬂuid sinks to

**take its place, setting up a convection current that circulates the energy. Radiation needs no particles**

at all; every object emits infrared radiation, which is how the Sun's energy crosses empty space to reach

us.

Houses are designed around these three roads. Loft insulation traps air, which is a poor conductor;

draught excluders block convection loops; shiny foil behind radiators reﬂects radiation back into the

room.

**U N I T 3 · T O P I C 3.6**
## Cooling by evaporation

**Learn**

**After swimming, you shiver even on a warm day. The water on your skin is evaporating: its fastest-**

moving particles escape into the air as vapour. Only the speediest particles can break free, so when they

leave, the particles left behind are the slower, cooler ones. Your skin loses energy and you feel cold.

Evaporation is therefore a cooling process. It happens at any temperature, unlike boiling, but it speeds

up when the surface is warm, when the surface area is large, and when the air above carries the vapour

away, as it does on a windy day.

Living things exploit this. Sweating cools you because evaporating water carries thermal energy away

from your skin; dogs pant to evaporate water from their tongues; even a wet clay pot keeps its contents

cool in hot climates by the same trick.
