## Welcome

This book is your Year 8 Physical Education course. It is written for Lower Secondary students who
already have Year 7 foundations and are ready for systems, race plans and more honest match play.

Across three terms you will study six sports. Each unit starts with rules and safety, then builds
techniques, then asks you to use those techniques in games. Fitness, cooperation and fair play run
through every page. The aim is not only to win the lesson. It is to leave Year 8 able to join a game, look
after yourself and help a teammate.

A Year 8 sports hall holds more than one sport. The habit that travels is how you arrive, listen and try the next skill.

## Safety and kit

Physical Education is safer when the rules are boring and everyone keeps them. Year 8 sports add nets,
runways, throwing sectors and contact-free rugby. The habits below apply to every unit.

**Habit**
**Why it matters**

Jewellery off, hair tied, laces double-
knotted

Fingers, eyes and ankles are hard to replace.

The right shoe for the surface
Studs on a hard court and socks on a polished studio are both
mistakes.

Water before you are thirsty
Outdoor running and indoor jumping both dry you out.

Tell the teacher if you are hurt
Playing on is not bravery when the next person inherits the injury.

Stop the game when someone is down
The score can wait.

**WATCH OUT**

A throwing sector, a long-jump pit and a rugby grid all look empty until someone walks into them.
Look before you collect, rake or chase.

**COACH'S TIP**

If you are not sure whether the space is live, treat it as live.

## Keeping score and keeping peace

Year 8 students are expected to run parts of their own games. That is a skill. A good official is loud
enough, still enough and honest enough that the next point can start.

**VOLLEYBALL**

Say the score before the serve. Call the rotation.
Replay if you are genuinely unsure.

**TENNIS**

The receiver's call on a line is accepted. The server
says the score.

**ATHLETICS**

A foul is a fact. Measure the legal mark. Do not
negotiate the tape.

**RUGBY**

Show the touch count. A two-handed touch is the
whole decision.

**TRY THIS ALL YEAR**

Volunteer to score one game per unit.

Practise the voice: score first, then the restart.

If two people disagree, replay. The lesson is longer than the argument.

## 1.1 Introduction and rules

Volleyball was invented in 1895 by William G. Morgan in Massachusetts as a less rough indoor game
than basketball. FIVB now governs the international sport. A school match is six players a side. A team
may play the ball three times before it must cross the net. A block contact does not count as one of
those three in the full game.

Rally scoring is used: every rally wins a point. A set is usually first to 25, win by two. You rotate
clockwise when you win the right to serve. Year 8 volleyball is not Year 7 passing with a louder shout. It
is a system: someone receives, someone sets, someone attacks.

The court is 18 m by 9 m. The attack line sits 3 m from the centre line. Antennae mark the crossing
space. Trainers, a raised net and a ball that is not soaked are the kit. Jewellery off. Nails short.

Net, antennae and the attack line: the three marks you must be able to name before the first serve.

**KEY POINTS**

Six players, three contacts, then over.

Rally scoring: every rally is a point.

Rotate when you win the serve.

Antennae, attack line and serving order are not optional decorations.

## 1.2 Warm-ups

A volleyball warm-up wakes the shoulders, ankles and the small steps you will use under the ball. Jog,
then dynamic swings, then passing on the move while you can still talk.

Include a few easy block jumps and a short reaction game. If the first contact of the lesson is a full
spike, the session started too late.

Warm-up movement should look like the game: short steps, hands ready, eyes on the next ball.

**KEY POINTS**

Pulse raiser, then shoulders and calves.

Passing on the move before isolated hitting.

A few controlled jumps, not a dunk contest.

Check the floor for dust and stray balls.

## 1.3 Ball control

The forearm pass (the platform) is still the most important Year 8 skill. Thumbs together, elbows
straight, angle the platform. The ball should rise in a friendly arc to the setter, not a rocket at the
ceiling.

The overhead set is a catch that is not a catch: contact above the forehead, both hands, a quick spring.
A double-hit set is usually two different sounds. Listen.

A still platform sends the ball where the setter already is, not where you wish they were.

**KEY POINTS**

Platform: flat, still, angled to the target.

Set: above the forehead, two hands, one sound.

Move your feet first. Hands last.

A playable pass is better than a heroic one.

## 1.4 Attacking skills

Attacking in Year 8 is approach, jump, contact, land. The last two steps are quick-quick. Contact the ball
in front of the hitting shoulder. Snap, do not hook.

A tip is an attack. When the block is huge or the set is tight, a soft touch into space is the intelligent
shot. The landing is part of the skill: two feet, knees soft, ready for the next ball.

The last two steps create the height. The landing protects the next rally.

**KEY POINTS**

Approach, jump, high contact, soft landing.

A tip is a decision, not a panic.

Hit the ball, then look for the next one.

Do not start the approach if the set is already past you.

## 1.5 Defensive skills

Defence starts before the opponent hits. Ready stance: knees bent, weight forward, hands in front. A
dig is a platform under pressure. Get the ball up, not pretty.

Blocking is a job, not a jump. Take away one angle. Hands reach over, not back. The player behind the
block must be ready for the deflection.

A block that takes one angle gives the diggers a smaller problem.

**KEY POINTS**

Ready before the hit, not after.

Dig: get it up.

Block one angle, not the whole net.

Cover the block.

## 1.6 Game strategies

Year 8 offence needs a designated setter. A 5-1 or a simple 6-6 still works if everyone knows who takes
the second ball. Receive in a W or a compact three. Do not all stand on the attack line.

On defence, talk the free ball. A gentle return is an invitation to run a full three-contact attack. On a
tough serve, the job is only to keep the ball off the floor.

A receive shape with a named setter turns three random contacts into an offence.

**KEY POINTS**

Someone is the setter. Everyone else knows it.

Receive shape before the serve.

A free ball is an attack opportunity.

Seams between passers are where serves live.

## 1.7 Game scenarios

Play short, spoken scenarios: side-out at 24-24, receiving after a rotation error, playing with a
substitute who has just come on. Keep them to one problem.

Conditioned games build habits: three contacts required, or no jump attack, or the setter must touch
every second ball.

Close sets reward the team that still passes to the setter.

**KEY POINTS**

One constraint per game.

Speak the score before the serve.

Rotate substitutes with a job.

Reset the serving order after every stoppage.

## 1.8 Cool-down and reflection

Cool down shoulders, calves and the lower back. Volleyball is jumps plus sudden stops.

Reflection is a team act. Who talked, who hid, and what the next match needs more of. Write one

Tennis is a net game invented in its modern form in nineteenth-century England and now governed by

Service boxes and the two widths of sideline: the court is a set of decisions, not a rectangle.

**KEY POINTS**

Serve diagonally, behind the baseline.

Singles is the inner court.

## 5.1 Introduction and rules

Rugby grew in nineteenth-century English schools. The World Rugby laws describe the full contact
game. Year 8 at this school plays touch rugby: a two-handed touch replaces the tackle. The ball may not
be thrown forwards. A try is scored by grounding the ball over the try line.

A typical school game is six or seven a side on a marked grid. Six touches, then a turnover, is a common
condition. Mouthguards if the department asks. No boots with dangerous studs on a hard surface.
Jewellery off.

Cones, a try line and a size 4 ball: the field is small so the decisions stay close.

**KEY POINTS**

Pass backwards or flat, never forwards.

Two-handed touch, then play the ball.

Six touches then turnover, unless the teacher says otherwise.

A try is a ground, not a dive at someone's knees.

and finishes with fingers pointing at the target. The ball must travel backwards relative to the passer.

A pop pass is shorter and faster for a support runner close in. Switch-pass work so both sides of the
body can give the ball.

Fingers at the target after a spin pass. The ball is already someone else's problem.

**KEY POINTS**

Two hands to catch.

Spin pass: hip to target, ball goes backwards.

Pop pass for the close runner.

Both sides, not a favourite hand only.

## 5.5 Defensive skills

Defence is a line. Players stay connected, move up together, and make a two-handed touch on the ball

A connected defensive line leaves no easy split pass. The touch is the full stop.

**KEY POINTS**

A connected line, not a collection of chases.

Two hands, then reset.
Show them to the side.

Talk the next receiver.

## 6.1 Introduction and rules

Sports aerobics is a judged fitness sport in the FIG family of gymnastics. In school it is a way to train
rhythm, strength, flexibility and performance without a full apparatus hall.

partner, not a race. Year 8 routines are a little longer than Year 7, but quality of shape still beats a long
sequence that falls apart.

A clear floor, a speaker and enough space: the equipment list is short on purpose.

**KEY POINTS**

Control first, then speed.

Land softly, joints stacked.

Music has counts. Learn them.

Know your personal square.

These words are used in the units. The definition matches the way the book uses the word.

**Attack line**
The three-metre line in volleyball that limits back-row attacks.

**Deuce**
A tennis score of 40-40. The game then continues until one player leads by two points.

**Drive phase**
The first low, powerful steps of a sprint after the start.

**FIG**
Federation Internationale de Gymnastique, the international gymnastics federation.

**Fix**
In rugby, to commit a defender so a teammate becomes free.

**Free ball**
A gentle volleyball return that lets the receiving team run a full offence.

**Overlap**
An extra attacker outside the last defender.

**Personal best**
Your own best legal mark, compared with yourself, not with the year.

**Platform**
The flat surface made by the forearms in a volleyball pass.

**Pop pass**
A short rugby pass to a support runner close to the ball carrier.

**Rally scoring**
A system in which every rally wins a point, not only the serving side.

**Sector**
The safe marked area in which a throw must land.

**Split step**
A small hop just as the opponent hits, so you can push off in any direction.

**Talk test**
A simple check: if you can speak a sentence, the endurance pace is about right.

**Touch rugby**
A rugby game in which a two-handed touch replaces the tackle.

**Turnover**
When the other team is given the ball, after six touches or an error.

## For teachers

This Student Book covers the Year 8 Physical Education course as mapped from the department source
file: Volleyball, Cross Country, Tennis, Athletics, Rugby (touch) and Sports Aerobics. Each unit keeps
the source topic list. Teaching prose is original.

Assessment pages match the source policy: collective game assessment, three fitness tests, and a
written paper when practical evidence is missing. Term dates and event days, if the school names them
later, can be announced in class. They are not printed here as if they were already fixed.

Coach's tips are written in a staff voice without naming individual teachers. The printed title is Physical
Education, Year 8.

Body type is 12 pt Source Sans 3. Do not shrink the type to fit a lesson onto fewer pages. Split the task
instead.
