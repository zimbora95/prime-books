## Come into the studio

**The studio, early on a bright morning. Look at the long table under the wide window, and at**
what each of the four is already busy with.

There is a room at the edge of a wood. It has one wide window, a floor of
bare boards, and a long wooden table with marks all over it.

On the table there are jars of water, and brushes standing up in a pot, and a
lump of grey clay under a damp cloth. Paintings lean against the wall,
waiting to dry.

## This book is about making things.

Four animals work in this studio. They push their fingers into clay. They look
very hard at a beetle and draw it. They press ink onto paper and lift a print
off. They mix two colours and find a third.

Sometimes what they make is not what they meant to make. That happens
to every artist. They look at it, say what they like about it, and try again. You
are going to do the same.

## Looking after your tools

A brush that is looked after will last for years. A brush left standing in water is
ruined in a day, because the hairs bend and never come straight again.

Three things to do with every brush, every time you finish.

Never stand a brush on its hairs, even for a minute, and never wash one in
hot water. Both of those bend the hairs, and a bent brush will not come to a
point again however carefully you treat it afterwards.

**1 Rinse**

Rinse the brush in cool
water until the water
runs clear.

**2 Shape**

Squeeze the hairs gently
to a point with your
fingers.

**3 Lay it down**

Lay it flat, or stand it
hairs UP in a pot.

**H O W D O YO U K N O W ?**

Look along the class brushes. Which ones still come to a point? How do
you know which of those were the ones left standing in water?

## Looking after the clay

Clay is different. Clay only stays soft if it stays damp, so it lives wrapped in a
wet cloth inside a bag. Left out on the table overnight it goes hard, pale and
crumbly, and it cannot be used again.

**Putting the clay away so it is still soft tomorrow. Look at the cloth going round the lump**
before the bag closes, and at the flat bag beside it with the air pressed out.

The bag is the one that catches a class out. Air left inside it dries the clay from
the middle, where nobody can see it happening, and no amount of water
brings it back.

**1 Wrap**

Wrap the clay in a damp
cloth.

**2 Press**

Press all the air out of
the bag.

**3 Store**

Keep it away from the
window.

## Paint, paper and clay together

**Four layers, in this order. Dry the tile hard, paint it one colour, tear the paper, then glue it on**
and draw on top. The order matters.

**L E T US LO O K C LO S E LY**

A dry clay tile is a surface, like a sheet of paper. You can paint on it, and you
can stick things to it.

## Sticking torn paper onto a picture is called collage. Using paint and paper
## and pencil all in one picture is called mixed media, which just means more
than one material.

Paint the tile first and let it dry. Then stick the paper on. If you do it the other
way round the paper goes soggy and lifts off at the edges.

Clay is dug out of the ground. It is made of tiny bits of rock, ground down so
small that water can slide between them, which is why wet clay moves under
your hands.

As clay dries, that water leaves. The bits pull closer together, so the pot gets a
## little smaller. Clay shrinks as it dries.

If one part dries faster than another, the two parts pull against each other
## and the clay cracks. That is why a pot dries slowly, under a cloth, away from
the window.

Dry clay is still soft enough to break. To make it hard for ever it has to be
## fired, which means baked in a very hot oven called a kiln. Your teacher does
the firing.

## Painting in layers

**L E T US LO O K C LO S E LY**

## A layer is one coat of paint, dried before the next one goes on top.

Waiting is the whole skill. Paint a pale wash and let it dry properly, and the
next colour sits cleanly on top of it. Paint on it while it is still wet, and the two
run together into mud.

You can put other things in the layers too: torn paper, pencil, a print. Using
## more than one material in one picture is called mixed media.

Pale layers first, dark ones last. It is easy to paint dark over pale, and nearly
impossible the other way round.

## About this book

## How the book is built. Four units, twenty topics, taken from the scheme of
work supplied by the school. Each topic runs to several pages: a looking-and-
learning page, then pages of practice. Every topic carries at least twelve
practice items, one modelled attempt worked through to a stated finding, at
least one How do you know? prompt, and at least one plate that SHOWS the
making rather than describing it. Every unit ends with a word list, a review, a
page for pupils to record what they can now do, and a project.

## Why the modelled attempt matters most. At six, watching one thing be
made is most of the teaching. Each Watch me try panel works a single
problem through to a printed result, and it is nearly the only place in this
book where an answer is given. Everything else is left open, because in Art &
Design a printed answer would often be a printed opinion. The closed
questions that do have answers are in the Answers section.

## Reflection is the assessment. There is no test in this book and no mark
scheme. What a pupil can SAY about their own choices is the evidence, and
the three questions on the assessment spread (what can I see, how was it
made, what do I think) are the instrument. Ask them in that order: a pupil
asked what they think first will say "it is good" and stop.

## Safety. Practical work is written for six and seven year olds under direct
supervision. Carry out your own risk assessment first and follow your school
policy. Three rules are absolute in this book: an adult loads, fires and
empties the kiln; no pupil uses a cutting blade, so relief blocks are built up
rather than cut away and vegetables are halved by an adult in advance; and
clay is sanded damp, never dry, because dry clay dust should not be
breathed.

## About this book, continued

## Misconceptions this book tackles head on. That a print comes out the
same way round as the block, when pressing reverses it (Unit 3). That a
monoprint can be printed twice (Unit 3). That clay neither shrinks nor cracks,
and that air-drying clay is waterproof (Unit 1). That white paint makes a
colour deeper, when it lightens it (Unit 4). That shadows are always grey and
the sky is always blue (Unit 4). In each case the wrong idea is named, put in a
character's mouth or inside a question, and then tested, because a
misconception that is never spoken aloud is never given up.

## The square codes. Every code in this book opens a museum collection, a
gallery page or a short film, and every destination was fetched and read
when this edition went to press. They are addressed to the adult holding the
book, or to the whole class with the code on the board, because nobody in
Year 2 owns a phone. The address is printed in words beside every code, so a
page still works with no device at all.
