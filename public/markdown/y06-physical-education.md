About this book

This is the Year 6 Physical Education student book in the Prime Books collection. It is
written for pupils of about ten to eleven, and for the teachers and families who work
with them.

Year 6 is the last primary year. The book consolidates the movement, teamwork and
fair-play habits built from Year 1 to Year 5, and it prepares pupils for the extra
independence of lower secondary Physical Education without turning into a secondary
textbook.

On these pages pupils practise technique, coordination, agility, speed and endurance.
They learn to use space, make decisions under a little pressure, talk clearly to a
teammate, lead a short activity, and judge their own work. Fitness is taught through
doing: warm-up, cool-down, how hard a task feels, water, rest, and the habit of being
active most days. Sportsmanship is treated as a skill, not a poster.

Book description. Physical Education Year 6: The High Fields is the final primary title in
the Prime Books Physical Education sequence. Six units take pupils through a five-bar
gate, a dry stone wall, an orchard, a hay meadow, a tack room and a hill spring. They
keep a broad range of skills in repair, name what they see, invent more than one
answer, take a real role in a team, share kit fairly, and plan activity they can keep. The
book is for the last year of primary school and for the first step towards the next one.

Up at the high fields

You have been doing this subject for six years, so let us skip the part where somebody
tells you that moving is good for you.

Here is what is actually different this year.

Up to now, a task arrived and you did it. The teacher set the distance, the teacher set
the apparatus, and the teacher decided when it was hard enough. This year you start
setting some of that yourself, and you find out that choosing well is a skill of its own,
and a harder one than it looks.

That is why the fields at the top of the hill are the ones you have been walking past
since Year 1 without going in.

STAY SAFE

Before anybody moves, check four things and say them out loud:

The ground. Dry, clear, nothing to trip on, nothing to slide on. The edges.
Nothing hard within two paces of any line you might run past, and nobody
working close to the wall. The gate and the apparatus. Every gate, bench, box
and mat checked and tested by an adult, not by you. A gate is for climbing only
when an adult says it is. The plan. Everybody knows which place they are going
to and what happens there.

If any of the four is wrong, you tell an adult and you do not begin.

DID YOU KNOW?

Rule 7 is new this year, and it is worth knowing why it is in the list at all.

A gate only works if the bar is honest. A pupil who says "bar 4" and quietly does
bar 2 has not gained anything: they have thrown away the one piece of
information the whole method depends on, which is where their limit actually is.
Nobody can help you at a level you are pretending to be on.

Saying "I am on bar 2" out loud, in front of five other people, is genuinely harder
than doing bar 4 badly. That is why it is a rule and not a suggestion.

U N I T 1 - TO P IC 1 . 1

Keeping a broad range in repair

△
BEFORE YOU GO UP

Name three things your body does well.

Now name the one it used to do well and has not done since about February.

Almost everybody has one. A skill you were good at, stopped using, and quietly
lost. Nothing dramatic happened to it: you simply stopped spending time on it,
and a skill you do not spend time on goes rusty in a way that is very hard to notice
from the inside.

This unit starts there, because consolidating a broad range means keeping all of
it in working order, and that is a completely different job from getting better at
your best thing.

■
THE EQUIPMENT YOU NEED

Two cones, chalk or tape for one line, a stopwatch or a wall clock, one clipboard and a pencil. Work
in threes.

○
WARM UP

Walk briskly anywhere on the clay ground for one minute, changing direction
whenever you meet somebody, without touching anybody. Then, on the spot:
ten shoulder circles backwards, ten ankle rises, and five slow squats to about
halfway down.

Now stand still. Your body is warm, your ankles and knees are awake, and you are
ready. Cold practice is wasted practice, and at this age it is also how people hurt
themselves.

You do not have one skill. You have a repertoire: a whole set of movements you can
already perform, built up over six years.

A repertoire behaves like anything else you own. The parts you use stay in working
order. The parts you stop using go rusty, and they go rusty silently, because nothing
warns you about a skill you are not practising.

So Year 6 asks for a different kind of practice from Year 3. Not refine my best movement.
Keep a broad range in repair.

Which means, before anything else, finding out what state the range is actually in. That

DID YOU KNOW?

A triathlon is three unalike movements in a row: at the standard Olympic
distance, a 1.5 kilometre swim, a 40 kilometre ride and a 10 kilometre run.

The interesting part is how it is timed. A triathlon is timed in five sections, not
three. Between the swim and the ride there is T1, and between the ride and the
run there is T2, and World Triathlon publishes each of those as its own column in
the results. As the rules put it, the time spent in transition is a timed segment and
it counts towards the overall finishing time.

So the sport has decided, officially, that the join is part of the event. Two
athletes can swim, ride and run at exactly the same speed, and the one who is
better at the two joins wins.

DID YOU KNOW?

Artistic gymnastics scores a routine with two numbers, and they measure exactly
the two things this topic separates.

The D score is difficulty. It adds up the hardest elements, and it pays a bonus
called Composition Requirements for using skills from the required element
groups, worth 0.5 each up to 2.00. It pays a second bonus, Connection Value, for
linking elements together. In other words, the D score pays for variety and for
joins, which is Topic 1.2.

The E score is execution, and it works the other way round. Every routine starts at
10.0, and judges take deductions off: 0.1 for a small error, 0.3 for a medium one,
0.5 for a large one, and 1.0 for a fall. Pausing between skills that were meant to be
connected loses connection value.

So a gymnast is paid for variety and linking, and charged for everything that is not
fluent. That is not a coincidence. It is the same judgement this unit is teaching
you to make.

The pedagogical spine

The unit rests on two moves.

The gate. Every substantial task is written four or five times over, each version harder
than the one below. The pupil chooses. This is not differentiation-by-worksheet: it is
the scheme's own word, self-selected, made into a routine, and it means a class of
mixed ability works on one task rather than three.

The four jobs, rotating. Mover, timer, watcher and setter, as in Year 5. The watcher's
job grows this year: it is no longer enough to describe a movement accurately, they
must turn the description into a success criterion another pupil could apply. That is
objective 2.3 in embryo, and it is deliberately started here.

Groups of three are therefore not a suggestion for topics 1.1 to 1.3, and topic 1.4 needs
four, because it adds a second mover.

Timing

Four topics and the project fill about six or seven lessons of 45 minutes for most
classes. Topic 1.1 needs a full lesson to itself: the audit takes longer than teachers
expect, and cutting it removes the evidence the rest of the unit depends on. Topic 1.3
needs a stopwatch per group and will not work without one, because its entire
argument rests on timing each movement separately.

Assessment

Assess from the Look what I can do list, the How did it go? rings, and the project's day

Two specific warnings.

Adaptations

Do not reward a high bar. A pupil at bar 2 who says so, works at bar 3 and reports
where it fails has met 1.4 better than a pupil failing at bar 5. Rule 7 in Getting set up
exists to protect that, and it only works if the marking backs it.

Mark the plan. Half of the project is the board, and the board is the part that is still
on the floor at the end of the lesson. If you only mark one thing, mark that.

Distances, heights, weights and timings are all adjustable. The objective is
control, not size. Shorten the run, lower the apparatus, reduce the count.

The gate is the adaptation. A pupil who needs an easier task takes a lower bar on
the same gate, which means they are doing the same lesson as everybody else
rather than a different one. That is the whole reason the book is built this way.

A pupil who cannot travel takes the same four families into seated or supported
movement: an arm pathway can be fast, slow, high, low, large, small, strong or light,
can be linked to a turn of the head or a reach, and can be timed for fluency exactly as
a run can. The five families become four; say so rather than pretending otherwise.

A pupil who cannot write dictates the watcher's description and the criterion to a
partner, or draws the marked ground instead. The criterion matters; the handwriting
does not.

Apparatus is set out and tested by an adult, always. The violet Stay safe panel in
1.4 is written to the pupil, but the responsibility is the teacher's, and 1.4 is the topic
in this unit where two movers share one space.
