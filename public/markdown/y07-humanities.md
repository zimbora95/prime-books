Introduction

This book tells one story, and it starts a very long way back.

Most humanities books begin with people. This one begins about thirteen thousand eight hundred
million years earlier, because people are late arrivals and it helps to know what they arrived into.

So part one builds the stage. You will start with the whole Universe, come in past our own galaxy to
the Sun, then to the planets, then to this one, then inside it, then out across its oceans and continents.

By the end of unit 5 you will be standing on a particular piece of land, knowing how it got there, what
the weather is like, and who else lives on it.

Part two brings on the players. Our species walks out of Africa, survives an ice age, learns to farm, and
then, in four separate corners of the world,

mountain. These are not four separate books stapled together. They run in order, on one timeline, so you

can always answer the question, "and what was happening everywhere else?"

Why the whole world, and not just part of it

Because it is true. Civilisation is not something that happened in one place and spread. It started

separately, several times, on different continents, in people who had never heard of each other and who
solved the same problems in different ways. A pupil who learns only one of those stories has been given a

smaller world than the real one.

You are reading this in a school in Portugal, and many of you were not born here. So Portugal appears
in this book the way any country should appear in a world history: as one place among many, on the
**same line of time as everywhere else. Look for the gold-topped Portugal at this stage panels. They are**

short on purpose. They are there so that when you read about the pyramids going up, you also know
what was happening on the Atlantic coast in the same century.

What this book expects of you

Three things, and none of them is memory.

**First, look before you read. Nearly every page has a picture, a map or a diagram with a caption that**
tells you what to notice. Look at it first. Reading the words afterwards is much easier.

**Second, ask how we know. This subject is not a list of facts to be believed. It is a set of conclusions**

drawn from evidence, and the evidence is always in the room. When this book tells you the Universe had
a beginning, it also tells you how anybody could possibly know that.

**Third, be willing to be wrong. Every unit has a Careful panel naming the mistake almost everybody**
makes. Making it is fine. Making it after you have read the panel is a waste of a good panel.

T O PI C 1 . 1

A story with a beginning

How can anybody know?

Here is a fair objection, and you should make it. Nobody was there. There were no people, no planets, no

atoms. So how can a book tell you what happened thirteen thousand eight hundred million years ago and
expect to be believed?

The answer is the same one a detective gives. You do not need a witness if you have enough evidence,
and the evidence has to fit together. Nobody watched the crime, but the fingerprints, the footprints and

the timing all point one way, and no other story explains all three at once.

Astronomers have three big pieces of evidence, and the useful thing about them is that they were
found separately, by different people, looking for different things. Any one of them on its own would be
interesting. All three together are hard to argue with.

There is one more thing that makes all this possible, and it is strange enough to be worth a paragraph of
its own.

Light is fast, but it is not infinitely fast. It travels about 300 000 kilometres every second. That is quick
enough that in a classroom you notice nothing at all. But space is very big, so light takes real time to

cross it. Sunlight takes eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach you. The Sun you can see is always the
Sun as it was eight minutes ago.

Push that further and it becomes a superpower. A galaxy a thousand million light years away is
showing you what it looked like a thousand million years ago, because that is how long its light has been

travelling. Astronomers do not have to imagine the young Universe. They can point a telescope at it,
because looking far away is looking back in time.

C AR E FU L

**"A light year" sounds like a length of time. It is not. It is a distance: how far light travels in one**
year, about nine and a half million million kilometres. Saying "it happened four light years ago" is

like saying "it happened four kilometres ago".

T O PI C 1 . 2

The Big Bang

What actually happened

About 13 800 million years ago, everything that now exists was packed into a space smaller than a full

stop, and unimaginably hot. Then it began to expand, and it has been expanding ever since. That
expansion is what we call the Big Bang.

In the first fraction of a second the Universe grew enormously. Within about three minutes it had
cooled just enough for the first simple centres of atoms to form: hydrogen, and a quarter as much helium.

And then, for a very long time, nothing much happened at all. The Universe was a hot fog, and light
could not travel through it any more than a torch beam travels through thick milk.

About 380 000 years in, it cooled enough for that fog to clear. Light escaped in every direction, and it
is still travelling. That escaping light is the faint glow astronomers can still detect today from every part

of the sky. It is the oldest thing anybody has ever seen.

Not an explosion

Nothing flew outwards into empty space. Space itself got
bigger.

the centre.

The bit everyone gets wrong

**The Big Bang was not an explosion in space. It was an expansion of space.**

That sounds like word games, but it matters, and there is a good way to picture it. Imagine a loaf of
raisin bread rising in an oven. Every raisin moves away from every other raisin, not because the raisins

are flying about, but because the dough between them is getting bigger. Sit on any raisin you like and you
will see all the others moving away from you, with the far ones moving fastest.

T O PI C 1 . 3

How galaxies were made

Gravity, given enough time

After the fog cleared, the Universe was almost, but not quite, smooth. Some regions had very slightly

more gas in them than others. Very slightly. A few parts in a hundred thousand.

That was enough. Gravity is a rule with no exceptions: every piece of matter pulls on every other

piece. So the slightly denser patches pulled a little harder, gathered a little more gas, and became denser
still, which made them pull harder again. Given a hundred million years, small differences become

enormous ones.

Where the gas piled up tightly enough, it grew so hot in the middle that it started to shine. That is a
star. Where a great many stars formed near each other, held together by their combined gravity, you get a
galaxy.

What a galaxy is

A galaxy is not a thing you could ever visit the edge of. It is a vast collection of stars, gas and dust,

spinning slowly, held together by gravity. The smallest hold a few million stars. The largest hold
thousands of millions of millions.

There are roughly two thousand million galaxies in the part of the Universe we can see, and each one
has, on average, something like a hundred thousand million stars. Do not try to feel the size of that.

Nobody can. Just notice that when this book says "our galaxy" in the next topic, it means one out of two
thousand million.

C AR E FU L

Stars are not scattered evenly through space, and neither are galaxies. Gravity clumps things. Stars
gather into galaxies, galaxies gather into groups, and groups gather into clusters. The Universe has

a structure, a bit like a sponge: long strands of galaxies with huge empty spaces in between.

The Milky Way, our home

Finding our own address

Our galaxy is called the Milky Way. It is a spiral: a flat disc with curved arms, a bright bulge in the

middle, and something like two hundred thousand million stars.

It is about 100 000 light years across. Light, the fastest thing there is, takes a hundred thousand years

to cross it.

Our Sun is not at the centre. It sits about halfway out, in one of the spiral arms, going round the
middle once every 225 million years or so. The last time the Sun was in this part of its orbit, dinosaurs

had not yet appeared.

T O PI C 1 . 5

Black holes

What one actually is

A black hole is not a hole. Nothing has been removed. A black hole is the opposite: it is matter, packed

so tightly into so small a space that its gravity, close up, becomes overwhelming.

Gravity depends on two things: how much mass there is, and how close you are to it. Squash the same

mass into a smaller ball and you can get much closer to its middle, so the pull you feel at the surface
becomes far stronger. Squash it enough and you reach a point where nothing that comes too near can get

out again. Not a spaceship, not a signal, not even light.

The boundary around it is called the event horizon. It is not a surface you could touch. It is simply the
line beyond which leaving is no longer possible.

T O PI C 2 . 1

The Sun, our own star

An ordinary star that happens to be ours

The Sun is a star. It looks different from the others only because it is close: about 150 million kilometres
away, while the next nearest star is 270 000 times further off.

It is a ball of hot gas, mostly hydrogen, 1 392 000 kilometres across. You could fit about 1.3 million
Earths inside it. It contains more than 99 per cent of all the material in the Solar System; everything else,

all eight planets, every moon, every asteroid, is made from the leftovers.

In its core, under enormous pressure, hydrogen is being squeezed together into helium. That releases
energy, and that energy is why the Sun shines. It has been doing this for about 4 600 million years and
has enough fuel for roughly another 5 000 million.

Why it matters more than anything else in the sky

Almost every source of energy on Earth is the Sun, one step removed.

Sunlight lands on a leaf, and the plant uses it to build itself. An animal eats the plant. You eat the

animal, or the plant. Your dinner is sunlight that has changed hands two or three times. Coal, oil and gas
are the same thing much older: ancient plants, made of ancient sunlight, buried and squeezed.

The Sun also drives the weather. It heats the tropics more than the poles, which makes air move,
which is wind. It evaporates water from the sea, which becomes cloud, which becomes rain. Take the

Sun away and within days the Earth would be dark, still and frozen.

C AR E FU L

The Sun is not on fire. Fire is burning, which needs oxygen, and there is no oxygen in space. The

Sun is doing something quite different: squeezing small atoms together into bigger ones and
releasing energy. That is called fusion, and it is why a star can last thousands of millions of years

while a fire lasts an evening.

The Moon

One quarter of the way to being a double planet

The Moon is 3 475 kilometres across, about a quarter of Earth's width, and 384 400 kilometres away.

Compared with other moons in the Solar System it is enormous relative to its planet.

The best explanation for where it came from is dramatic. About 4 500 million years ago, when the

Earth was young, something roughly the size of Mars hit it. The collision threw an enormous amount of
molten material into orbit, and that material gathered together and cooled into the Moon.

Two pieces of evidence support this. The rocks brought back by the Apollo missions are chemically

very like Earth's outer layers and unlike most other bodies. And the Moon has a very small iron core,
which is what you would expect if it formed from the outer layers of a planet rather than from scratch.

half we can see from where we are standing.

moon. When the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, the lit side faces us fully: a full moon. In

Tides, and why they matter

The Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth, and pulls hardest on the water nearest to it, because gravity
weakens with distance. That makes the sea bulge slightly on the side facing the Moon, and, for the same

reason in reverse, on the opposite side too. As the Earth turns, coasts pass through those bulges, which is
why most places get two high tides a day.

The Sun pulls too, less strongly. When the Sun and Moon line up, the two pulls add together and tides
are unusually large. When they are at right angles, the tides are unusually small.

**The Moon does not have a permanent dark side. It has a far side, which we never see from Earth**
because the Moon turns once for each orbit. The far side gets exactly as much sunlight as the near

side; we simply never see it lit, because we never see it at all.

skin of an apple.

**The mantle is by far the biggest layer, nearly 2 900 kilometres deep. It is rock, and it is hot, and here**

is the part that matters: over very long periods it flows. Not like water. More like extremely stiff treacle,
or a glacier. Centimetres a year. But it flows, and that flow is what moves the continents.

**The outer core is liquid iron and nickel, about 2 200 kilometres thick. Because it is liquid metal and it**
swirls, it generates the Earth's magnetic field, which is what makes a compass point north and what

shields us from much of the Sun's radiation.

**The inner core is a ball of iron about 2 400 kilometres across, at roughly 5 200 degrees C, which is**

about the temperature of the Sun's surface. It is solid, despite the heat, because the pressure on it is so
enormous.

of wave arrives nowhere in a large "shadow" region on the far side of the Earth: it had been stopped by

something liquid.

T O PI C 3 . 2

Rodinia, Pangaea and the wandering continents

The map has not always looked like this

Look at a world map and find the eastern coast of South America and the western coast of Africa. They

fit. Not roughly: closely, like two pieces of a jigsaw.

People noticed that as soon as decent maps existed, and for centuries it was treated as a coincidence.

In 1912 a German scientist named Alfred Wegener said it was not a coincidence, and collected evidence:
identical fossils of the same land animals on both coasts, matching rock formations, and glacier scratches

in places that are now tropical.

He was laughed at, largely because he could not say what moved the continents. He was right anyway.
The mechanism, the slow flow of the mantle, was worked out about fifty years later, long after he had
died.

Four maps, hundreds of millions of years apart

The continents have joined and split several times. Two of those arrangements have names worth

knowing.

**Rodinia existed about 1 100 million years ago: one great landmass, before there was any life on land at**

all.

**Pangaea formed about 335 million years ago. All of today's continents were joined into a single mass,**
surrounded by a single ocean. You could have walked from what is now Portugal to what is now Florida

without getting your feet wet.

Pangaea began to break up about 175 million years ago, and the pieces have been moving ever since.

They have not stopped. The Atlantic is getting wider by about 2.5 centimetres a year, and Australia
moves north by about 7 centimetres.

Plates that push, pull and slide

The crust is broken, and the pieces move

The Earth's outer shell is not one continuous skin. It is broken into about fifteen large pieces, called

tectonic plates, plus many small ones. They float on the flowing mantle beneath, and they move.

Everything interesting happens at the edges. In the middle of a plate the ground is quiet. At a plate

boundary you get mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes, deep ocean trenches and rift valleys, and which of
those you get depends entirely on how the two plates are moving relative to each other.

**Pulling apart. The plates move away from each other and hot rock rises to fill the gap, cooling into new**

which makes a deep trench in the sea floor, melts rock on the way down and sends it back up as
volcanoes. If both are equally buoyant continental crust, neither goes down and the rock crumples

upwards instead. That is how the Himalayas are being built, as India pushes into Asia at about 5
centimetres a year.

T O PI C 3 . 4

When the Earth moves

Earthquakes

An earthquake is what happens when strain that has been building for years is released in seconds. The

rock either side of a fault is locked by friction, the plates keep trying to move, the strain grows, and
eventually the rock breaks and both sides lurch into a new position.

The energy travels outward as waves. Their size is measured on a scale where each step up is about
thirty times more energy released, so a magnitude 7 is not slightly worse than a magnitude 6; it is

roughly thirty times worse.

Almost nobody is killed by the ground shaking. People are killed by buildings. That is why the same
magnitude of earthquake can kill a handful of people in one country and tens of thousands in another:
the difference is building standards, and building standards cost money.

Volcanoes

A volcano is a place where molten rock from below reaches the surface. Most of them sit exactly where

the plate map says they should.

The clearest example is the Ring of Fire, a horseshoe around the edge of the Pacific Ocean where

about three quarters of the world's active volcanoes and about 90 per cent of its earthquakes occur. It is
not a mystery; it is the outline of a plate.

Somebody once said that calling this planet Earth was a mistake, and that it should have been called

Ocean.

They had a point. Nearly 71 per cent of the surface is sea. The average depth of that sea is 3 688

metres, which means that if you added up all the space where living things can live, on land, in the

T O PI C 4. 1

Meet the five oceans

One ocean, divided into five for convenience

There is really only one ocean. All the water is joined; a bottle dropped off Lisbon can wash up in the

Caribbean, and a current leaving the Atlantic can end up in the Pacific. The divisions are made by
people, for convenience, and they follow continents and, in one case, a current.

Since 2000 the internationally recognised list has five names on it.

**The Pacific is the biggest, at 165.2 million square kilometres. It is larger than all the land on Earth**
put together, and it holds the deepest known point anywhere, the Challenger Deep, at 11 034 metres.

**The Atlantic is second at 106.5 million square kilometres. It is the one Portugal faces, it is the**
youngest of the big oceans, and it is still getting wider.

**The Indian is third at 70.6 million square kilometres, and the warmest. It drives the monsoon that**

decides whether a fifth of the world's people have a good year.

**The Southern Ocean, 21.9 million square kilometres, is the one most adults never learned. It rings**
Antarctica, and it is defined not by land but by a current: the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, which
flows all the way round the world without ever meeting a coast.

**The Arctic is the smallest at 14.1 million square kilometres, the shallowest and the coldest, and much**

of it is covered by floating sea ice.

Salt

Sea water is about 3.5 per cent salt, which means roughly 35 grams in every litre. If you evaporated all the

rock, and rivers carry those minerals to the sea. Water leaves the sea again by evaporating, but the salt
does not evaporate with it, so it accumulates.

The great conveyor

Water that goes somewhere

Ocean water does not sit still. It moves in currents: enormous, steady rivers within the sea, some of them

carrying a hundred times more water than every river on land put together.

Two things drive them. Near the surface, wind. The prevailing winds drag the top of the ocean along

with them, and because the Earth is spinning, the water curves as it goes, which is why the big surface
currents run in great loops.

Where surface water gets very cold and very salty, near the poles, it sinks, and other water flows in to
replace it. This drives a slow global circulation that takes a thousand years to complete one loop.

T O PI C 4. 4

The ocean makes the weather

Two towns at the same latitude

Here is a fact that ought to be impossible.

Lisbon is at about 38.7 degrees north. So is Washington DC, in the United States, and so, roughly, is
Beijing. All three are the same distance from the equator, so all three get almost exactly the same amount

of sunlight over a year.

Their winters are not remotely alike. Lisbon's coldest month averages about 11 degrees C.
Washington's averages about 2 degrees. Beijing's averages about minus 3.

The difference is the ocean, and it works in two ways.

**First, water heats and cools far more slowly than land. A coast is therefore milder than an inland**
place at the same latitude: cooler in summer, warmer in winter, because the sea beside it changes

temperature slowly.

**Second, currents carry heat. The Gulf Stream carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico across the**

Atlantic towards Europe. The air passing over it picks up that warmth and carries it onto the land.
Western Europe is several degrees warmer in winter than it has any right to be for its latitude.

And the opposite case

Currents can work the other way. The Humboldt Current brings cold water up the west coast of South

America, and the air above cold water holds little moisture, so it produces almost no rain. That is a large
part of why the Atacama Desert in Chile is the driest place on Earth: some weather stations there have
never recorded rainfall at all.

Cold water is also rich in nutrients, so the same current supports one of the world's great fisheries. A

cold current can give you a desert on land and a feast in the sea, at the same place, at the same time.

POR T U GAL AT T H I S STAGE

Portugal is an Atlantic country in a way that shows up in the weather every single day. The Azores
High, a large area of high pressure over the mid Atlantic, sits over the country in summer and gives

it dry, sunny months. In winter it shifts and lets Atlantic depressions through, which is why almost
all Portuguese rain falls between October and March.

The sea also decides where people go. The cool Atlantic keeps Porto and Lisbon milder in August

than Madrid, four hundred kilometres inland, and the same Atlantic swell produces the enormous
waves at Nazare, where a submarine canyon focuses ocean energy onto one small stretch of coast

and where the largest waves ever surfed have been recorded.

**Question. Nuuk in Greenland and Oslo in Norway are both at about 60 degrees north. Oslo's**
coldest month averages about minus 4 degrees C; Nuuk's averages about minus 8. Suggest a reason.

**Thinking it through. First, rule out the obvious. Same latitude, so the same sunlight. That cannot**
be the answer.

Second, look at what is upstream of each one, in the ocean. Oslo is on the European side of the
North Atlantic, downstream of the warm current that crosses from the Gulf of Mexico. Nuuk is on

the Greenland side, where a cold current comes down from the Arctic.

Third, check the direction of the winds. In these latitudes the prevailing winds blow from the west,
so each place gets air that has just crossed the water to its west. For Oslo that water is warm. For

Nuuk it is cold.

**Answer. The difference is the ocean, not the Sun. Oslo receives westerly air warmed by a warm**

Atlantic current; Nuuk receives air chilled by a cold Arctic current. Same latitude, different
neighbouring water.

How to read a map

**The key explains the colours and symbols. Never guess. Blue usually means water, but on a rainfall**

**The scale tells you how far it really is. It is usually a line marked with a distance. Two maps on the**

Every flat map of a round world is wrong

You cannot flatten a sphere without stretching something. Try it with an orange peel and you will see
the problem in ten seconds.

So every world map has to choose what to get wrong. The common one in classrooms keeps the
shapes of countries roughly right, which makes it good for navigation, but it badly stretches areas far

from the equator. On it, Greenland looks about the size of Africa. Africa is in fact about fourteen times
bigger.

That is not a conspiracy. It is geometry. But it is worth knowing, because a map that quietly makes
some parts of the world look bigger than they are will quietly change what you think is important.

C AR E FU L

The maps in this book are equirectangular: straight lines of latitude and longitude. They keep
directions simple, which is right for a first world map, and they stretch areas near the poles, which

is why Antarctica looks enormous. No map escapes this. Always ask what a map has chosen to get
wrong.

The monsoon

Much of southern and eastern Asia lives by the monsoon: a seasonal reversal of the wind. In summer the

national emergency for several countries at once.

Antarctica

A continent with no countries

Antarctica is 14.2 million square kilometres, larger than Europe, and it has no permanent population at

Where we start

T O PI C 6 . 2

Living through the Ice Age

A colder world

For most of the time people were spreading across the planet, the world was in an ice age. The last cold

peak was about 20 000 years ago, and it changed the map.

Enormous ice sheets covered northern Europe, northern Asia and much of North America. So much

water was locked up as ice that sea level was about 120 metres lower than today. Land that is now sea
floor was dry: Britain was joined to Europe, Asia was joined to North America at the Bering land bridge,

and it was possible to walk between islands that are now separate.

That lower sea level is a large part of how people reached the Americas and Australia at all.

How people lived

They hunted and they gathered. Groups were small, perhaps twenty to fifty people, and they moved with

the seasons, following herds and ripening plants.

They were extremely good at it. They knew which hundred plants in their area were edible and which
were poison, when each one fruited, how to track animals, how to make fire, how to sew fitted clothing
from hides using bone needles, and how to make stone tools with edges sharper than surgical steel.

They also made art. The cave paintings of western Europe, at Lascaux, Altamira and Chauvet, are

between 17 000 and 36 000 years old: horses, bison, lions and rhinoceros drawn with real skill by people
who had never seen writing.

POR T U GAL AT T H I S STAGE

Portugal holds one of the most important rock art sites in the world, and it was very nearly

drowned.

In the Coa Valley in the north east, thousands of animal figures were carved into open-air rock
faces, many of them between about 22 000 and 10 000 years old: horses, aurochs, ibex and fish.

They are outdoors, not in a cave, which is rare and which makes them fragile.

They were found in 1994, during work on a dam that would have flooded the whole valley. After a

long public argument the dam was stopped, the valley was protected, and in 1998 it became a
UNESCO World Heritage site. It is a useful reminder that heritage is not automatically saved.

Somebody has to decide.

The great change

Farming

Around 11 500 years ago, as the climate warmed, something happened in south west Asia that had never

happened before: people began deliberately planting the seeds of the plants they wanted, and keeping the
animals they ate.

It happened separately in at least eleven different places around the world, with different crops each
time: wheat and barley in south west Asia, rice and millet in China, maize, beans and squash in Central

America, potatoes in the Andes, sorghum in Africa, taro in New Guinea.

That "separately" matters. Nobody taught anybody. Groups of people who had never heard of each
other reached the same idea within a few thousand years, because the same warming climate made it
possible in each place.

Ancient Egypt lasted about three thousand years as a recognisable civilisation.

To feel how long that is, count backwards. Three thousand years before today is 1000 BC, which is

How does anything last that long? That is the question this unit answers, and the answer is mostly

T O PI C 7 . 1

The river that made a country

A green line through a desert

Egypt is almost entirely desert. Yet for three thousand years it was one of the richest places on Earth, and
the reason runs straight through the middle of it.

**The Nile is about 6 650 kilometres long and flows north, from the highlands of east Africa to the**
Mediterranean. That surprises people, because north is up on a map, but rivers flow downhill, not down

the page.

Every year, summer rains far to the south swelled the river until it overflowed its banks in Egypt, from
about June to September. When the water went down it left behind a layer of dark, fertile silt.

**The Egyptians called that strip kemet, the black land: a ribbon of superb farmland, in places only a**
**few kilometres wide. Beyond it was deshret, the red land, which was desert and where nobody could live.**

Almost every Egyptian in three thousand years lived within sight of the river.

T O PI C 7 . 3

Gods, temples and the afterlife

Many gods, and one big idea

**The Egyptians had hundreds of gods. Ra the sun god, who crossed the sky each day. Osiris, god of the**

**dead and of rebirth. Isis, his wife, a goddess of magic and protection. Anubis, with the head of a jackal,**
**who looked after the dead. Thoth, with the head of an ibis, who invented writing.**

**Behind them all stood one idea: maat. It is usually translated as truth, balance, order, justice, and it**
means all of those at once. Maat was the way the world was supposed to be: the river rising, the sun

returning, the strong not robbing the weak. The pharaoh's central duty was to uphold maat, and every
Egyptian was expected to live by it.

Why they built for the dead

Egyptians believed that death was a passage rather than an ending, and that a person needed their body,
their name and their possessions to continue.

So they preserved bodies by mummification: removing the organs, drying the body with natron salt

for about forty days, then wrapping it in linen. They buried the dead with food, furniture, tools and
jewellery. And they carved the person's name, because a name that was spoken was a person who
continued.

At the judgement, the Egyptians believed, the dead person's heart was weighed against the feather of

maat. If the heart was heavy with wrongdoing, it failed. If it balanced, the person went on.

C AR E FU L

It is easy to describe Egypt as a civilisation obsessed with death. That gets it backwards. Almost

everything that survives from Egypt is from tombs and temples, because those were built of stone in
the dry desert, while houses, including the pharaoh's, were built of mud brick beside the river and

have washed away. We are not seeing what Egyptians cared about most. We are seeing what
happened to last.

China's Sorrow

The Yellow River carries so much silt that it drops it on the river bed, which slowly rises. People build
banks to hold the river in; the bed rises further; the banks are raised again. In places the river now runs

above the level of the surrounding fields, held in by its own banks.

China's Sorrow.

This produced something politically important. Controlling that river needed work on a scale no

village could manage: dykes, dredging and canals, organised across whole regions. A strong central
government that could organise flood control was not a luxury in China; it was the difference between
eating and starving. The very earliest Chinese legends are about a hero who tamed the floods, and they

were remembered for a reason.

**Paper, recorded from about 105 AD, was cheap. Before it, writing went on bamboo strips, silk, clay or**

makes open ocean sailing possible, and everything in the age of exploration follows from that.

which it was not. Fireworks came first, then rockets, bombs and guns. It made stone castle walls obsolete,
and with them a whole way of organising society.

Goods went both ways. Out of China: silk, paper, porcelain, tea. Into China: horses, glass, gold, wool,
spices. But the important cargo was not goods at all.

**Ideas travelled. Buddhism reached China along these routes and became one of its major religions.**

Paper-making spread west, reaching the Islamic world in the eighth century and Europe centuries later.
Astronomy, mathematics and medicine moved in both directions.

**And so did disease. The Black Death, which killed perhaps a third of Europe's population in the**
fourteenth century, is generally thought to have travelled west along these same routes. A network that

carries silk carries everything else too.

The wall that was many walls

The Great Wall is not one wall either, and it was not built all at once. Different dynasties built different
walls in different places over roughly two thousand years. What tourists visit today is mostly Ming

dynasty work from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

Its purpose is usually misunderstood. It was never intended to be an impenetrable barrier; a
determined army could always get through or round it. What a wall does is force raiders to come through
the gates, and raiders who have to use gates can be taxed, counted, controlled or fought at a place of the

defender's choosing. A wall with a garrison is not a shield. It is a filter, and a signalling system: beacon
towers could pass a warning along the frontier far faster than a horse.

AMAZI NG B U T T R U E

The claim that the Great Wall is the only human structure visible from space is false, and it has
been publicly denied by astronauts who have looked. The wall is long but narrow, roughly the width

of a road, and the same colour as the ground it stands on. Motorways and cities are far easier to see.

T O PI C 1 0. 1

The Andes, a vertical world

Straight up

The Andes run about 7 000 kilometres down the western side of South America, the longest mountain

range on any continent. They were pushed up, and are still being pushed up, where the Pacific sea floor
is forced under the continent: the same process you met in unit 3.

In the middle section, in Peru and Bolivia, the range is wide and high, with a plateau called the
altiplano sitting at around 3 800 metres. Lake Titicaca, on that plateau, is the highest large navigable lake

in the world.

Being that high changes everything. There is about a third less oxygen in the air at 4 000 metres than
at sea level. It is cold at night all year round. Ultraviolet light is fierce. And crops that grow at one height
will not grow five hundred metres higher.

Several climates on one mountainside

**The key idea of Andean geography is that climate here changes with altitude, not with latitude. Go up a**

mountainside and you pass through as many climate zones as you would by travelling thousands of
kilometres north.

**The Maya lived in what is now southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras. Their great cities,**

What they had instead

**The quipu. Instead of writing, the Inca recorded information on knotted cords. A main cord carried**

hanging strings, and the type of knot, its position along the string and the colour of the cord all carried
meaning. Quipus certainly recorded numbers: populations, harvests, taxes, llamas. Whether they could

also record language is still argued about by scholars. Officials called quipucamayocs were trained to make
and read them.

**The mita. Instead of taxes in money, people owed the state labour: a certain number of days a year**
building roads, terraces, temples or serving in the army. In return the state was expected to feed you in a

bad year from its storehouses. It was an exchange, and when the Spanish later kept the mita and dropped
the obligation to feed people, it became something much worse.

**Reciprocity. Andean society ran on the ayllu, an extended family group that held land in common and**
worked each other's fields in turn. The empire was built on top of that habit rather than against it.

Terraces, roads and runners

Three problems, three solutions

Everything the Inca built was an answer to a specific mountain problem.

**Problem: mountainsides are too steep to farm, and rain washes the soil away.**

**The answer was terracing: cutting the slope into steps held by stone walls. It is much more than a flat**
surface. Behind the wall the Inca layered gravel and sand for drainage, then carried topsoil up by hand.

The stone wall absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night, protecting crops from frost, so a
terrace can grow crops that would die on flat ground at the same height.

**The answer was the chasqui, relay runners stationed a few kilometres apart. Each ran his stretch at**

That is not decoration. Mortar cracks in earthquakes, and this is earthquake country. Interlocking
blocks can shift slightly and settle back. Spanish buildings put up on top of Inca foundations have fallen
down in earthquakes while the Inca walls beneath them stood.

Cuzco, Machu Picchu and the end

The centre of the world

**Cuzco was the Inca capital, at 3 400 metres, and it was the point from which the empire's four parts**

**Machu Picchu sits on a ridge at 2 430 metres above the Urubamba river. It was probably built around**

stone channel from a spring, and stonework of the highest quality.

It was never found by the Spanish, which is why it survived. It was known to local farmers all along,
and it was brought to international attention in 1911 by the American explorer Hiram Bingham, who

What this book assumes

It assumes no prior humanities knowledge beyond a primary programme, and no mathematics beyond

division and percentages. It assumes English as the language of instruction, and it assumes that a
significant number of pupils are reading the subject in an additional language, which is why the body

type is set a full point above the Year 7 floor, the measure is short, and panel names are one or two plain
words.

Where Portugal fits

Portugal has no chapter in this book, and that is a decision rather than an omission. The pupils are

international, and Portuguese history taught as a separate block invites them to file it as somebody else's
subject.

**Instead there are ten short Portugal at this stage panels, one in each unit, each placed inside the**
world period it belongs to and each between 80 and 150 words. A pupil reading about the Great Pyramid

meets, on the same spread, the walls of Zambujal, begun in the same century. The intended effect is that
Portugal is normal rather than special: one place among many, on one timeline.

What changed from the first edition

The first edition was an eighteen page, two unit sample built on a different plan, with Unit 1 as

"Geography and you". It is kept in _superseded_2026-08-07/ and nothing has been overwritten.

The scope is wider in both strands, on the argument set out in the curriculum recommendation filed
in the project hub: geography now begins with the Universe rather than with map skills, and history
now covers four continents rather than the usual one or two. The Input document's chapters on the Inca,
China and Egypt are all carried forward, reordered chronologically and rewritten from scratch. Its
chapter on the European Union and the United Kingdom is not carried forward as a chapter; its map

skills survive as topic 5.1 and its European geography as topic 5.4.

Assessment

**Every topic ends with a numbered Your turn set, easiest question first. Closed questions have answers at**

the back; open questions have acceptance criteria, which is what a good answer must contain rather than
the words it must use. Marking to acceptance criteria is slower than marking to a key and it is the only

honest way to mark an argument.

**Each unit ends with What you can now do, which doubles as a self-check and as the source for the**

knowledge organiser pages that fill what would otherwise be blank versos.

What this book does not do

It does not teach the science of climate change, ecosystems or natural hazards in depth. Those are taught

in far greater scientific detail in the Science programme, and duplicating them here would waste pages
this subject needs.

It does not pretend that every question has a settled answer. Where scholars disagree, for instance
about whether quipus recorded language, the book says so.

It does not treat any civilisation as the normal one against which others are measured. That is the

single reason the geography strand comes first: a pupil who knows where the Andes, the Nile and the
Huang He are does not need to be told that civilisation happened in more than one place.
