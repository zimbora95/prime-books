UNIT 1 · SESSION 1.1

Reading a travel narrative

Learn

Travel writing is non-fiction, but it uses the tools of fiction: vivid detail, a distinct voice, and a shape that carries the

reader along. The best travel writers are not the ones who go furthest. They are the ones who notice most.

✦
G E T T I N G STA RT E D

Think of a journey you remember clearly, however short. In pairs, discuss:

what one detail do you remember most sharply, and why that one?

what did you notice that a stranger might have missed?

how would you make a reader feel they were there with you?

❝
E X T R A CT · ' T H E S L E E P E R TO P O RTO '

The night train pulled out of Lisbon at ten, and for the first hour I could still read the city in

the window: warehouses, a football pitch lit up and empty, the long orange smear of a

motorway. Then the buildings thinned, and the dark came in properly, and the glass

became a mirror instead of a window.

Across the aisle, an old man was eating an orange with a penknife, working the peel off in
one unbroken spiral. He did it with the unhurried competence of someone who has done it

ten thousand times. He offered me a segment without looking up, and I took it, and neither

of us said anything at all.

I slept badly and gratefully. Somewhere past Coimbra the rhythm of the rails changed

pitch, and I woke to a grey window and pine trees streaming past, and that particular ache

behind the eyes that means you have arrived somewhere before you are ready for it.

What is the effect of the phrase "the glass became a mirror instead of a

window"? What does it tell us about the passage of time and the narrator's focus?

1

The writer describes the old man in detail but gives him no dialogue.
Explain how this choice shapes our impression of him.

2

"I slept badly and gratefully." This pairing seems contradictory. What does
it suggest about the narrator's state of mind?

UNIT 1 · SESSION 1.2

Figurative language

Learn

Figurative language means saying one thing in terms of another. A metaphor states the comparison directly. A
simile signals it with like or as. Personification gives human qualities to something that is not human.

In the extract, "the long orange smear of a motorway" is a metaphor: the road is not literally a smear, but the word
captures speed, blur and colour in three syllables.

Find one detail that could only come from someone who was actually there.
Explain why it feels authentic.

R E A D I N G T I P

When a writer chooses an unexpected word, pause on it. In "badly and gratefully", the surprise is the whole

point: it holds two truths at once. Unexpected words are where meaning is densest.

UNIT 1 · SESSION 1.3

Writing an account

Learn

A good account of a journey does not report everything. It selects. Choose three or four moments that carry the
feeling, and let the rest go.

a. "the dark came in properly"

b. "pine trees streaming past"

c. "the long orange smear of a motorway"

Write your own metaphor for each of these: a crowded bus; rain on a window;
the moment a train stops.

2

Explain why "streaming" is a better choice than "moving" or "going".

travel writing: non-fiction about a journey, using vivid personal detail.

metaphor: describing something as if it were something else.

simile: a comparison using like or as.

personification: giving human qualities to a non-human thing.

present perfect: a tense linking a past action to the present moment.

Plan an account of a real journey. In your notebook, note:
1

the three moments you will actually describe

one small, specific detail for each moment

one figurative phrase you will use

the feeling you want the reader to be left with

Write your account, in 250 to 350 words. Use the simple past as your base
