**Welcome to Year 9 Art & Design**

Art is everywhere.

It can be found in paintings and sculptures, but also in photographs, films, buildings, clothes,
posters, illustrations, objects, digital images, installations and the spaces around us. Sometimes
art is carefully planned. Sometimes it is spontaneous. Sometimes it is beautiful. Sometimes it is
strange, uncomfortable, confusing or even difficult to understand.

You already know how to make images. Year 7 trained looking and the visual elements. Year 8
trained portrait, architecture and the design brief. Year 9 asks a harder question: why you make a
particular choice, and what that choice communicates.

Art and Design is not only about drawing well or producing something that looks attractive. It is
about observing, questioning, interpreting, experimenting, making decisions and communicating
ideas. Through experimentation and reflection, an uncertain starting point can become something
much more developed.

Artists do not simply ask: “How can I draw this?” They also ask:

• What am I trying to communicate?
• Why have I chosen this subject?
• What am I trying to say?
• What choices could I make?
• What happens if I try something different?

These questions are at the heart of artistic practice.

**How artists work**

Most artists do not jump straight to a final masterpiece. A common studio cycle is:

1. Notice something (an object, a feeling, another artwork, a place).
2. Collect (sketches, photos, notes, material tests).
3. Try (rough versions, mistakes, surprises).
4. Choose (what to keep, what to change).
5. Refine (improve control, composition, meaning).
6. Share (show, title, explain).
7. Reflect (what did I learn?).

Warm-ups that look wrong can still be excellent practice. Neatness is not the same as learning.

**What does it mean to think like an artist?**

Artists do not simply ask: "How can I draw this?"

They also ask:

• What am I trying to communicate?
• Why have I chosen this subject?
• What does this image make me feel?
• What could this object represent?
• How can colour change the meaning of an image?
• What happens when I remove information?
• Can an ordinary object become visually interesting?
• How can I make the viewer look at something differently?
• What can I communicate without using words?
• What happens when I combine two apparently unrelated ideas?
These questions are part of artistic thinking.

There is often no single correct answer in Art & Design. However, there are stronger and weaker
decisions. A strong artist is able to make decisions, test them, recognise when something is not working
and make changes.

Year 9 is the year you practise that independence. Your teacher will still demonstrate, still set briefs, still
keep the room safe. The thinking, though, has to become yours.

**Remember**

**Looking is a method**

Looking is not the same as seeing. We see thousands of things every day, but we do not always really
observe them.

When you draw from observation, you are not simply copying an object. You are learning to notice
proportion, relationships, subtle changes in colour, light and shadow, surface, movement, negative
space and unusual viewpoints.

When you look at an artwork, move through five stages. Year 7 and Year 8 used a four-step look. Year 9
adds evaluate and respond, because you are now expected to take something into your own work.

1. DESCRIBE. What can you actually see? Name colours, lines, shapes, objects, materials, scale. Do not
interpret yet.
2. ANALYSE. How has the artist organised the visual elements? Think about composition, contrast,
viewpoint, surface.
3. INTERPRET. What might the work mean? What feeling, idea or question does it suggest? Give a reason.
4. EVALUATE. What is successful or interesting? What would be weaker if it changed?
5. RESPOND. What quality will you borrow as a strategy, not as a costume? What will you try this week?

**Chapter 1 - Introduction to still life**

**Why arrange objects at all?**
A pile of shopping on a table is not yet a still life. A still life begins when someone decides.

You decide which objects belong together. You decide what is in the foreground. You decide where the
light comes from. You decide what is cropped out of the frame. Those decisions are already a kind of
thinking.

In the Dutch Golden Age, a breakfast piece might show bread, a pewter jug and a lemon. The objects
were ordinary. The painting was not: it could speak about plenty, time, or the shine of a surface that will
not last. You do not have to paint a skull to talk about time. A browning apple and a stopped watch can
do it.

Page
10

A still life is a work of art showing non-living objects. These items can be natural, like fruit and flowers,
or man-made, like books and cups. The word comes from an old Dutch term meaning quiet or stationary
life.
**Common subjects**
Natural things: fruit, vegetables, plants, shells and cut flowers. Man-made things: books, vases, glasses,
coins and pots. Symbolic items: skulls or candles, used to show that life is short.
**Why artists paint still life**
Focus on basics: artists study colour, shape and how light hits an object. Freedom: objects do not move,
so artists can take their time arranging them. Hidden meanings: some pieces send messages about
wealth, time or death.

**Composition**
Composition refers to the way visual elements are organised within an artwork.

Consider:

• Where is the main object?
• What is in the foreground?
• What is behind it?
• Where does the eye move?
• Are there areas of empty space?
• Are objects overlapping?
• Is the composition balanced or deliberately unbalanced?
• Where does the light come from?
A strong composition is not necessarily symmetrical.

Sometimes visual tension is created by placing an object close to the edge, using an unusual viewpoint
or leaving a large area of empty space.

**Possible effect**

**Artist references: four strategies, not four costumes**
Study how artists have used still life differently. Compare artists who use realistic observation with
artists who simplify, distort or abstract objects.

Ask:

• What objects have they selected?
• Why might those objects have been important?
• How have they arranged them?
• What is the role of colour?
• How is light used?
• What mood is created?
• What makes the work recognisable as a still life?
Year 9 uses four strategies. Steal the strategy. Do not copy the picture.

**Chapter 2 - Observational studies**

Before developing a final still life, you need to learn to observe.

Observation is different from drawing what you think an object looks like.

When drawing from observation, look carefully at proportions, angles, contours, negative spaces, light,
shadow, surface, texture and relationships between objects.

**Line**
Experiment with different types of line: continuous line, broken line, contour line, expressive line,
directional line, cross-contour line.

Do not automatically outline everything.

Sometimes an edge can be communicated through a change in tone rather than a drawn line.

**Tone**
Tone describes the range from light to dark.

Look for highlights, mid-tones, cast shadows, reflected light and deep shadows.

Avoid shading everything evenly.

Think about where the light is coming from and how it affects each surface. Glass collects highlights.
Cloth swallows them. A lemon has a core shadow that turns.

**Chapter 3 - Media experimentation**

Different materials produce different visual languages.

The objective is not to use as many materials as possible.

The question is: Which material best communicates the quality or idea I want?

**Pencil**
Explore pressure, hatching, cross-hatching, blending, erasing and directional marks.

A 2B maps. A 6B states a dark. An eraser is a drawing tool: it pulls light back out of graphite.

**Paint**
Consider transparency, opacity, brush marks, layering, colour mixing, and controlled versus expressive
application.

Watercolour can leave the paper as highlight. Gouache can sit on top like enamel. A dry brush can
become linen. A loaded brush can become a shine.

**Pastel**
Explore soft transitions, strong marks, blending, layering, texture and contrast.

Pastel dust is a surface. Fixative changes it. Do not blow pastel towards anyone's face.

**Mixed media**
Combining materials can create unexpected results: pencil + paint; pastel + collage; ink + watercolour;
drawing + photographic elements.

IMAGE 9.1.08 - Four media, one subject. Purpose: graphite, gouache, pastel and mixed-media versions of the same bottle and fruit.

**Pastel, paint and collage**
Share water pots. Do not wash acrylic or gouache into a sink in lumps. Keep pastel work away from wet
paintings. Collage adhesive wrinkles thin paper: test on a scrap.

Paraphrase. Name the work, the date if you have it, and the source. Then write what you notice.

## Unit 9.2 - Identity

Identity is the way we understand ourselves and how we are represented by others.

It can involve personality, memories, family, culture, place, interests, experiences, relationships,
possessions, appearance and emotions.

Artists have explored identity through portraits, self-portraits, objects, symbols, photographs,
environments and abstract imagery.

A portrait does not have to show someone's face.

An object, colour, photograph or place can sometimes communicate something about a person more
powerfully than a literal portrait.

is visible, and how much is constructed?

**Techniques 1 - Watercolour**

Transparent paint, water and control.

TECHNIQUE 1 - Flat wash, graded wash and wet-on-wet. Purpose:
show control of water.

**Materials**

- Watercolour pans or tubes, one round brush (size 8 or 10)
and a large flat wash brush

- Two pots of water: one for rinsing, one clean for mixing

- Cold-pressed (textured) paper, 200 gsm or heavier, taped
flat to a board

- Kitchen towel for lifting colour and controlling drips

**How to use it**

1. Tape the paper flat. Draw lightly in pencil first if the
composition needs it.

2. Mix more paint than you think you need, in a palette well,
before touching the paper.

3. Work light to dark: pale washes first, strong colour last. Let
each layer dry before the next.

4. Wash and dry the brush whenever you change colour.

**How to look after the images you make**

Label every study on the back or in the corner: date, materials, time taken and one sentence about what you learned. Keep flat
work in a folder, not folded in a bag.

Photograph anything three-dimensional, shiny or fragile straight away, in daylight, with the artwork flat and the camera parallel
to it. Scan or photograph prints before you glue them into the sketchbook, so a copy survives even if the original is damaged.

Keep your best result and your most interesting failure.

**Ways of working**

**Wet-on-dry**

Brush clean water onto dry paper, then touch colour into
it. The colour blooms softly with fuzzy edges. Good for
skies, backgrounds and mood studies. Control it by tilting
the board and by how wet the paper is.

**Graded wash**

Load the flat brush, sweep one even band across the
top, dip slightly, sweep again just below so the bands
merge. Each band is paler than the last. Good for skies
and simple backgrounds.

**Wet-on-wet**

Wet the whole area with clean water, then drop colour onto the wet surface so it spreads on its own. You steer it; you do
not draw with it. Good for loose studies of clouds, water and soft shadows.

**Techniques 2 - Block print**

Carve a block, ink it, press it.

TECHNIQUE 2 - Carving, inking and the finished print. Purpose: show
the reversed image.

**Materials**

- Soft rubber or lino block, and gouges (V-blade for lines,
U-blade for clearing)

- Water-based block-printing ink, a flat tray and a brayer
(roller)

- Smooth paper for printing and newsprint for test pulls

- A wooden spoon or baren for rubbing the back of the paper

**How to use it**

1. Draw your design, then transfer it to the block in reverse:
printed areas are what you leave raised.

2. Carve away everything that should stay the colour of the
paper. Always carve away from your hand.

3. Roll a thin, even layer of ink on the tray until it sounds
sticky, then roll it onto the block.

4. Lay the paper on top and rub firmly with the back of the
spoon before peeling it back.
**How to look after the images you make**

Label every study on the back or in the corner: date, materials, time taken and one sentence about what you learned. Keep flat
work in a folder, not folded in a bag.

Photograph anything three-dimensional, shiny or fragile straight away, in daylight, with the artwork flat and the camera parallel
to it. Scan or photograph prints before you glue them into the sketchbook, so a copy survives even if the original is damaged.

Keep your best result and your most interesting failure.

**Ways of working**

**Single-colour relief**

One block, one colour. Every mark you carve becomes a
white line; everything left raised prints as solid colour.
Strong, graphic, and the safest way to learn the process.
Print an edition of at least three identical pulls.

**Reduction print**

Print the whole edition in the palest colour first, then
carve more away from the same block and print the next
colour over it. Each step removes what you want to keep
from the previous colour. Plan it on paper first: the block
is destroyed as you go.

**Two-block print**

Carve a second block for a second colour (for example a background or a highlight) and print it in register on top of the
first. Use small pencil registration marks so the layers line up.

**Techniques 3 - Monoprint**

One drawing in ink, one print only.

TECHNIQUE 3 - Drawing into the ink, pressing, pulling the single
print. Purpose: show the ghost mark.

**Materials**

- A flat, smooth plate: acrylic sheet, gel plate or a laminated
card

- Water-based printing ink or slow-drying acrylic, rollers and
cotton buds

- Paper, and a wooden spoon or press for even contact

- Cotton buds, rags, cocktail sticks and fingers for drawing
tools

**How to use it**

1. Roll a thin, even layer of ink over the plate.

2. Draw directly into the ink with a cotton bud (adds a line) or
add ink back with a fingertip (adds a mark).

3. Lay the paper over the plate and rub the back evenly with
the spoon.

4. Peel the paper back in one confident movement. This pull
is the artwork: it cannot be repeated.

**How to look after the images you make**

Label every study on the back or in the corner: date, materials, time taken and one sentence about what you learned. Keep flat
work in a folder, not folded in a bag.

Photograph anything three-dimensional, shiny or fragile straight away, in daylight, with the artwork flat and the camera parallel
to it. Scan or photograph prints before you glue them into the sketchbook, so a copy survives even if the original is damaged.

Keep your best result and your most interesting failure.

**Ways of working**

**Subtractive (drawing back)**

Roll ink over the whole plate, then remove the image by
drawing into the ink with cotton buds and rags. The lines
print as the colour of the paper. Fast and confident
marks work best: hesitation shows.

**Additive (drawing on)**

Leave the plate clean and paint or roll the image directly
onto it, then print. Allows colour and loose brushwork,
but the ink must still be wet when you pull the print.

**Ghost print**

Without re-inking, print the plate a second time. The faint leftover image is called a ghost. Print it, then draw into it with
pencil or pastel: many artists prefer the ghost to the first pull.

**Techniques 4 - Collage**

Cut, tear and layer paper into an image.

TECHNIQUE 4 - Selecting papers, layering and the finished collage.
Purpose: show torn and cut edges.

**Materials**

Keep your best result and your most interesting failure.

**Ways of working**

**Opaque layering**

Acrylic dries quickly and covers what is underneath.
Build the painting in layers: dark ground first, big shapes
next, lights and details on top. Mistakes can simply be
painted over once dry.

**Impasto**

Apply thick, undiluted paint with a brush or palette knife
so the marks stand up off the surface in ridges. Direction
matters: every stroke stays visible. Good for texture
studies and expressive surfaces.

**Dry brush and scumbling**

Wipe most of the paint off the brush, then drag it lightly across the texture of the surface. Broken colour shows through.
Good for weathered surfaces, which links directly to Unit 9.3.

**A - Visual elements**

The visual elements are the raw materials of looking and making. Year 7 introduced them. Year 8 asked
you to choose them. Year 9 asks you to choose them because they communicate.

When you write about work, try: "I used tone to..." not "I used lots of things."

IMAGE A.01 - Visual elements board. Purpose: a quick map of line, shape, form, colour, tone, texture, pattern and space.

**Line**
A mark that connects two points, or the edge where two shapes meet.

Line can be contour (edge), gesture (action), or implied. Weight, speed and direction change feeling. In
still life a cross-contour can wrap a bottle. In identity a continuous line can record looking without vanity.

**Shape**
A flat, enclosed area. Geometric or organic.

A bottle is a shape before it is glass. A cropped forehead is a shape. Negative shapes between objects
often tell the truth of an arrangement.

**Form**
Shape with volume. Light on one side, shadow on the other.

Clay masks are form. A shaded lemon is form on paper. Drawing creates the illusion of form. Sculpture
creates actual form.

**Colour**
Hue, value, saturation.

In identity work, colour is often a feeling before it is a local colour of skin or cloth. In still life, a limited
palette can be more eloquent than a full rainbow.

**Tone**
The scale from light to dark, with or without hue.

If everything is mid-grey, nothing sits forward. Find the darkest dark and the lightest light before you
decorate.

**Texture**
Tactile (real) or visual (drawn or photographed).

Year 9 surface work lives here: rust, peel, weave, dust. Mixed media can be texture, not only describe it.

**Pattern**
A motif that repeats.

Repeated photographs, window grids, a grid of objects (Milroy). Pattern can comfort or, if broken, can
wake the eye.

**B - Principles of design**

If elements are ingredients, principles are recipes: how you organise the elements.

IMAGE B.01 - Principles of design board. Purpose: balance, contrast, emphasis, rhythm, movement, unity, proportion, space as visual
diagrams.

**Balance**
Visual weight. Symmetrical balance feels stable. Asymmetrical balance can feel lively. A still life with all
objects on the right needs a counterweight: a shadow, a title, or empty space used on purpose.

**Contrast**
Difference: light/dark, rough/smooth, huge/tiny.

Contrast creates emphasis. Two identity portraits in opposite palettes are a contrast experiment.

**Emphasis**
The focal point: where the eye lands first.

If you have three emphases, you have none.

**Rhythm**
Repetition with beat. Vary the beat so it does not lull.

**Movement**
How the eye travels through the work. Diagonals, pointing objects, a large form leading to a small one.

**Unity**
Do the parts belong together? Unity is not sameness. A mixed-media surface piece has unity if every
layer has a job.

**Proportion**
Size relationships. Awkward proportion can be a mistake or a decision. Kahlo-like scale changes (a plant
larger than a head) are proportion used as language.

**Space (as a principle)**
What you leave empty, and why.

**Remember**
Elements = what you use. Principles = how you organise. In critiques, try: "I used contrast of tone to
create emphasis on the key."
