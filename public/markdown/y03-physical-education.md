About this book

This is the Year 3 Physical Education student book in the Prime Books collection. It
is written for children of about seven and eight, and for the teachers and families
who work with them.

Year 1 taught the fundamental movements themselves: run, jump, hop, skip, land,
stop, throw, balance. Year 2 took those movements onto a marked court and into
a team bib. Year 3 does not teach them again. It asks the child to refine, combine,
decide and look after what they can already do.

Across the year the book develops:

The six friends from Years 1 and 2 are still here: Pip, Bramble, Sorrel, Tuft, Willow
and Rowan. The ground has changed. It is a measured meadow track, and the
jobs on it are mover, timer and watcher.

Modified games appear inside the units: athletics, football, basketball, handball,
striking and fielding, gymnastics, dance, relays. This remains a Physical Education
book, not a specialist sports manual.

Book description

Physical Education Year 3 is the next book in the Prime Books Physical Education
line for Prime School. It follows the same six friends onto a new ground: a cinder
track at the edge of a meadow, where a movement is practised until it is smooth,
described until a teammate can use the words, and played until everybody in the
game has a job.

Children learn to combine skills, use simple tactics, take a short turn at leading,
share kit fairly, and notice what their own body is doing while it moves. Every
comparison is against their own earlier attempt. No task ranks one child against
another.

The book is for Year 3, ages seven to eight, and belongs on the same shelf as the
Year 1 and Year 2 Physical Education titles. Same collection. Same cast. Harder
work.

Welcome to the meadow track

You already know how to run.

You learned that in Year 1, out in the meadow, along with jumping, hopping,
skipping, landing softly and stopping where you meant to. Last year you took all of
it onto a marked court and played in a team.

So this year is not about learning new movements. It is about something harder
and much more interesting: doing a movement you already know, better than
you did it last week, and being able to say what changed.

That is what the white lines are for. A measured ground lets you find out.

✧
DID YOU KNOW?

Look down the Athletics Day column. The sprint is the only activity there
where being fastest is the whole answer. In the other four, a pupil who
moves smoothly and knows exactly what their body is doing will beat a pupil
who is simply quick.

That is not a comforting thing to say. It is the reason this book spends its first
ground on control rather than on speed.

<!-- page 11 -->

Here is the difference. In Year 1, "I can jump" was the whole answer. This year the
answer sounds more like this: I can land my jump without wobbling, and I can go
straight into a roll afterwards, and if you ask me to do it slowly I can do that too.
Same jump. Far more control.

The track before anybody arrives. Six lanes brushed flat, a rake against the fence and one stopwatch left hanging on the
rail. Look at the white lines: everything in this book happens between them.

How every task runs

Almost every task in this book has the same six steps. Learn them once and you
will never have to be told the order again.

The step people skip is 3, and skipping it costs the whole task, because a watcher
who does not know what to look at will fall back on saying "that was good".

If something is hard

Some of these tasks will be hard for you and easy for the person next to you, and
some will be the other way round. That is not a problem to be hidden. It is
information, and it is the only kind this book actually cares about.

Fetch. Read The equipment you need at the top of the topic and collect it
before anybody moves. One person fetches for the three of you.

1

Mark the ground. Chalk or tape the lines the task asks for. Walk the space
once, empty, and move anything you would rather not run into.

2

Agree the one thing. Say out loud what the mover is trying to change, and
what the watcher is going to look at. If you cannot say it in one short sentence,
it is not one thing yet.

3

Go. The mover moves, the timer counts or times, the watcher looks. Nobody
talks during a go except the timer, and the timer only says the number.

4

Swap. Every three goes, change jobs. By the end of the task all three of you
will have moved, timed and watched.

5

Put back. Equipment goes back where it came from, chalk gets wiped or the
tape lifted, and the space is left as you found it.

6

✧
DID YOU KNOW?

Rule 4 is the one everybody breaks, and it is worth knowing why it matters. A
timer who takes a tenth of a second off to be nice has not been kind: they
have thrown away the only evidence the mover had that their practice
worked. Numbers are how you find out. A kind number is a useless number.

▸ Learn

Doing a movement ten times is not the same as practising it.

Practice means repeating a movement with something you are trying to change.
Without that, you are only keeping fit, which is fine, but nothing gets smoother.

Three things make a practice work.

IT MUST HAVE
WHICH MEANS

One thing to change
Not "jump better". "Land with my knees bent and my hands still."

A way to tell
A watcher looking at that one thing, or a number from the timer.

Enough goes
Three attempts tell you nothing. Ten start to tell you something.

A movement that has been practised properly looks smooth: it has no wasted
wobble, no extra step, and no arm shooting out to save it. Smooth is not the same
as fast. Smooth usually turns out to be faster anyway, which surprises people.

◆
WHAT HAPPENED

and his hands go out sideways to stop himself falling over.

He does it nine more times. Every one of them is a big jump with a thump
and the hands going out.

Bramble says, "That was ten jumps." Willow says, "That was the same jump
ten times."

They are both right, and Willow's version is the useful one.

DID YOU KNOW?

Rosa Mota ran the marathon for Portugal. She won the Olympic gold medal

WORD
WHAT IT MEANS

practice
Repeating a movement with one thing you are trying to change. The noun: a good

practise
To do that repeating. The verb: I practise my landing.

refine
To make a movement you can already do better, in small steps.

smooth
A movement with no wasted wobble, no extra step and no arm shooting out to save
it.

wobble
Any small loss of balance that the body has to correct. Countable, which is why the

sequence
Two or more movements joined so that there is no stop in the middle.

join
The place where one movement becomes the next. The only new work in a sequence.

dynamics
The way a movement is done: its speed, level, size and force.

speed
How quickly a movement happens. One of the four dials.

level
How high or low the body is: on the toes, upright, or close to the floor.

How strong or how light a movement is.

pathway

apparatus
The equipment you move on, over and through: benches, mats, hoops, ropes.

To change small details of a movement so it still works on new ground.

This section is written to the teacher, not to the pupil. It is here because every
Prime Book carries it, and because a Physical Education book is impossible to run
from the pupil pages alone: the tasks assume groups of three, a checked space
and apparatus an adult has set out.

What this book is for

The whole book covers all 29 Year 3 Physical Education scheme objectives: Moving
Well (1.1 to 1.4), Understanding Movement (2.1 to 2.4), Moving Creatively (3.1 to
3.4), Taking Part (4.1 to 4.6), Taking Responsibility (5.1 to 5.5) and Healthy Bodies
(6.1 to 6.6). The Input assessment row, "Test term1, term 2 and term 3", is the
three review spreads in the back matter.

It deliberately does not re-teach Year 1 fundamental movements. Year 3 assumes
them and teaches refining, combining, deciding and looking after.

Modified sports sit inside the units, not as a separate sports textbook: athletics in
Unit 1, observation and tactics in Unit 2, gymnastics and dance in Unit 3, football,
basketball and striking-and-fielding as small-sided games in Unit 4, hockey and
racquet sharing in Unit 5, a fitness circuit in Unit 6.

What Unit 1 is for

Unit 1 covers the four Year 3 Moving Well objectives from the Year 3 Physical
Education scheme of work: refining basic movement skills through practice (1.1),
performing them sequentially (1.2), control and coordination with varied space,
speed and dynamics (1.3), and adapting to contexts, apparatus and equipment
(1.4).

It deliberately does not re-teach the movements themselves. Year 1 taught
running, jumping, hopping, skipping, landing, stopping, throwing and balancing,
and Year 3 assumes them. If a pupil cannot yet land softly or stop where they
meant to, the Year 1 material is the right place to go, not a slower version of this
unit.

The pedagogical spine

The unit rests on one move: the class is split into mover, timer and watcher,
and the jobs rotate. Everything else follows from it.

Groups of three are therefore not a suggestion. A pair loses the watcher, and the
watcher is where the learning is.

Timing

Four topics and a project fill about six lessons of 45 minutes for most classes.
Topic 1.1 is the one to give extra time: ten purposeful repetitions takes longer than
teachers expect, and cutting it to four removes the evidence the rest of the unit
depends on.

A practical note on the timetable. A lesson that starts at 14:30 in Portugal in
September is often the hottest part of the afternoon, and topic 1.3 asks pupils to
hold a fast setting for a full crossing. Where the hall is warm or the outdoor ground
is in full sun, run 1.3 in a morning slot and keep the afternoon for 1.1, which is
mostly standing still and watching.

It makes the scheme's observation and feedback objectives practisable in Year
3 rather than deferred to a written test.

It gives every pupil a number or a description that is about their own earlier
attempt, which is the only comparison this book permits.

It occupies the two pupils who are not moving, which is what usually goes
wrong in a primary Physical Education lesson.

Assessment

Assess from the Look what I can do list and the project's step 5 comparison, not
from the times. A slow pupil who holds a dial exactly where they put it has met
1.3. A fast pupil who cannot slow down has not.

The Input names three assessment points across the year, one per term. Those
become three review spreads in the complete book and are not part of this unit.

Six things go wrong in this unit in almost every class. None of them is a sign that
the unit is too hard, and five of the six are the lesson working rather than failing.

Distances, heights and timings are all adjustable. The objective is control,
not size. Shorten the lane, lower the apparatus, allow a longer run-up.

A pupil who cannot travel takes the same four dials into a seated or supported
movement: an arm pathway can be fast, slow, high, low, large, small, strong or
light exactly as a run can.

A pupil who cannot write dictates the watcher's description to a partner, or
draws the ground instead. The description matters; the handwriting does not.

Apparatus is set out and tested by an adult, always. The orange Stay safe
panel in 1.4 is written to the pupil, but the responsibility is the teacher's.

If you only have three lessons

Where time is short, teach 1.1 and 1.2 properly and treat 1.3 and 1.4 as one
combined lesson. Do not cut 1.1: it carries the practice method that every later
topic depends on, and a class that has not done ten purposeful repetitions has no
evidence to reason from in 1.2, 1.3 or 1.4.

If the hall is unavailable, all four topics run outdoors on any marked ground. Only
1.4 needs apparatus, and a chalked line, a low wall and two hoops will carry it if
benches and mats cannot be set out.
