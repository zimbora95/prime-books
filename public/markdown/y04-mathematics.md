Prime Books · Mathematics 4
Welcome

WE L C OME · C ONT INUE D

Last year you counted, added, took away and shared. You worked with numbers up to a
thousand, and you always started at zero and went upwards. This year the ground opens

up underneath zero.

You will meet temperatures in the mountains that fall below zero on a winter morning. You
will meet a car park two levels under a building. You will meet a reservoir whose water level

is measured downwards from a mark on a wall. In every case you need numbers that are

less than nothing, and a way of saying how far below zero they are.

You will also look much more closely at what a digit is worth. In 4 062 the 4 is not worth

four. It is worth four thousand, because of the column it sits in. Move that same 4 one

column to the right and it is worth only four hundred. Nothing about the digit changed: its
place changed.

Those two ideas, place and direction, are what Unit 1 is about, and they come back in every

unit after it.

☜Talk about it

Before you read on, talk to the person next to you. Where have you seen a number

with a minus sign in front of it, outside a maths lesson? On a fridge, a weather

forecast, a lift button, a news report? Collect three real places between you.

⚲Out in the real world

On the morning of 8 January the class rang the weather station at the
Torre, the highest point in mainland Portugal.

The reading was −6 °C. Two hundred kilometres to the south, in Faro, it was 13 °C at

the same moment. Ana wrote both numbers on the board and asked a question that

took the rest of the lesson: how far apart are they?

You cannot answer that by taking 6 from 13. The two readings are on opposite sides

of zero, so you have to travel up to zero first, and then onwards. That journey is what

this unit is about.

When you count on in hundreds, only the hundreds digit changes, until you cross into

a new thousand. When you count on in tens, only the tens digit changes, until you

Sequences and the term-to-term rule

▤What makes a sequence linear

A sequence is a list of numbers in order. Each number in it is called a term. The term-
to-term rule tells you how to get from one term to the next.

If the rule adds or subtracts the same amount every time, the sequence is linear: the

steps are all equal, and on a number line the jumps are all the same width. If the steps
change, the sequence is non-linear.

Square numbers and the patterns they make

▤Why they are called square

Take one counter. Now make the smallest square you can with counters: two rows of
two, which needs 4. The next square needs three rows of three, which is 9. These

numbers are called square numbers because that is the shape they make.

You write them as a multiplication of a number by itself: 3 × 3 = 9. The first six square
numbers are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36.

1 × 1 = 1
2 × 2 = 4
3 × 3 = 9
4 × 4 = 16

R E F E R E N C E
For teachers

Notes on this unit, collected here rather than printed on the pupils' pages.

✎For teachers

Sequencing. Part 1 deliberately introduces counting back past zero before Part 2 names negative
numbers. Pupils meet the idea as a movement they can perform, and only then meet the vocabulary.
If your class is confident, the two parts can be taught in one week; if not, Part 1 stands alone.

The Watch out! panels are the assessment map. Each one names a specific misconception: that
counting back stops at zero; that −7 is greater than −3 because 7 is greater than 3; that a zero digit
can be dropped; that going up in sixes is a rule. When pupils tick needs more work on the review
page, send them to the matching panel rather than to the whole part.

Part 3 and the syllabus. The scheme of work document repeats topic 1.2's objectives under the
heading 1.3. Part 3 of this book teaches the place value objectives that Year 4 requires: the value of
each digit in a four-digit number, composing and decomposing, figures and words, and comparing
and ordering to 10 000. If your copy of the scheme still shows the repeated text, this is the reading we
have used.

Practical work. The Part 2 thermometer readings are real in shape but were generated for this book.
Replace them with your own school's weather station data if you have a week of it: the questions are
written so that only the numbers change, not the tasks.

Marking the project. The published criteria carry thirty marks for explaining the six zeros, which is
more than for the plotting. That weighting is deliberate. A pupil who can say why the full mark on a
dam wall is a sensible zero has understood the unit; a pupil who plots beautifully without that has not.

Answers. The answer section was generated from the same source as the questions, so it is
arithmetically reliable. Where a question asks for an explanation, the answer gives the point to look
for, not a form of words to require.

This page may be photocopied for use with your own classes.
