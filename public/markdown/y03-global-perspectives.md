## About this book

Global Perspectives, Year 3, Student Book. Part of the Prime Books Subject Series
for Cambridge Primary.

This book is set in a shared garden. Six neighbours work one patch of ground
together: they plant, they argue gently about where the beans should go, they
notice what the others are doing, and they find out that a job looks different
depending on which end of the watering can you are holding. That is the whole
subject in one picture.

Across six units you will look at play, at what people need, at screens, at talking
and listening, at how things and people travel, and at families and celebrations.
## Each time you will do the same four things: ask, listen, compare, act.

## Introduction

Welcome to Year 3 Global Perspectives.

This is a book about looking outwards. Not looking down at a page, or up at a
teacher, but out at other people, and then back at yourself to see whether you
still think the same thing.

You will notice something early on. Most of the questions in this book do not have
one right answer waiting at the back. Some do: if you count how many pupils in
your class walk to school, there is a number and it is either right or wrong. Others
do not. Whether a village should spend its money on a football pitch or on a bus is
not that kind of question. You will meet both kinds, and part of the work is telling
them apart.

Here is how the book runs.

## Six units. Each one opens with a picture and a big question, and tells you what
you will be able to do by the end of it. Inside a unit there are numbered topics,
such as 1.1 and 1.2, so you always know where you are. The page number is at the
bottom of every page, and the contents at the front tells the truth about where
everything is.

## Four moves, over and over. Ask a question worth asking. Listen to what
someone else says, including the part you did not expect. Compare what you
heard with what you already thought. Act: do something small and useful with
what you now know.

## Three review spreads, one at the end of each term, to look back at what you
found out.

## Answers at the back, for the questions that have answers. Try first. An answer
you have argued your way to is worth ten you have copied.

One last thing. In this book you will be asked to talk to real people: pupils in your
class, your teacher, an adult at home, someone who works nearby. Real people
are not homework. Ask them when they have time, listen properly to what they

## Unit 1 · Sports and recreation

The Shared Garden · Bento, Matilde, Tomás, Leonor, Gonçalo and Inês

Bento has chalked a line on the stones and set up six wooden skittles. Everyone
wants to play. Almost at once there is an argument, and it is a good one: Leonor is
the smallest, so should she stand closer to the skittles than Bento?

Hold on to that question. It comes back on every page of this unit.

**WHAT THIS UNIT IS FOR**

## 1. Name games you play, and say what each one needs.

Some games need a ball. Some need six people. Some need nothing but a wall.

## 2. Compare indoor and outdoor games, and say when each one suits.

Weather, space and how many players you have all change what you can play.

## 3. Describe traditional games from Portugal and from other countries.

Old games travel, change their names, and turn up again somewhere else.

## 4. Explain what exercise does to your body, using what you have measured.

You will count your own heartbeats before and after moving.

## 5. Play safely, and spot what makes a place safe or unsafe.

Checking the ground before you run is a skill, not fussing.

## 6. Work in a team, and say what a good team member actually does.

Passing, waiting your turn and cheering all count as playing.

## 7. Decide whether a game is fair, and change one rule to include more
## people.

This is the hardest one, and it is the point of the unit.

## My favourite sport and game

**LOOK AROUND**

Think of a game you played this week. Not on a screen: a game with your
body, or with your hands. Who was with you? What did you need to play it?

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

## A sport is a game with rules, played for exercise or to win. Football is a sport.
So is swimming, and so is running.

## A game can be smaller than that. Hopscotch is a game. So is hide and seek.
You can invent a game in two minutes with a wall and a ball, and it will still
have rules, because you and your friends will agree them as you go.

Every game needs some things and not others. Football needs a ball, some
space and someone to play with. Hopscotch needs a flat piece of ground and
something to draw with. Chess needs a board, and it needs you to sit still and
think.

When you know what a game needs, you know when you can play it.

**UNIT 1 - TOPIC 1.4**

## Why do we exercise?

**LOOK AROUND**

Put two fingers on the side of your neck and keep still. Can you feel a small,
steady beat? That is your heart, moving blood around your body while you sit
perfectly still.

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

## Exercise is moving your body so that it grows stronger.

When you run, your muscles have to work harder, and working muscles need
more oxygen. Oxygen arrives in your blood, and your heart is the pump. So
your heart beats faster and you breathe faster. That is not something going
wrong. It is your body doing exactly the right thing.

Exercise helps in ways you can feel and ways you cannot see:

Your heart and lungs get stronger, so the same run feels easier next month.

Your muscles and bones get stronger, so you can carry, climb and balance
better.

Sleep often comes more easily after a day with movement in it.

## Playing safely

**OUT IN THE WORLD**

Gonçalo will not let anyone start until he has walked the whole playing area.
He finds a broken tile, a rake left lying with its teeth up, and a puddle. He
moves the rake, points out the tile, and says the puddle is fine because
everyone can see it.

Leonor calls him slow. Bento says Gonçalo has just saved somebody's ankle.

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

Playing safely is not the opposite of playing hard. It is what lets you keep
playing.

Before you start, three quick checks:

## The ground. Flat? Anything to trip on? Anything sharp?

## The space. Room to stop without hitting a wall, a road or another game?

## The kit. Laces tied, nothing loose, the right shoes for the surface?

While you play, two more:

## The rules. Everyone agreed them, so everyone follows them.

## Each other. If someone is hurt, the game stops. Every time.

## Teamwork in sports

**DO YOU REMEMBER?**

Think of a time a team you were in did well. What did the other players do

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

## A team is a group who play on the same side and depend on each other. That
last part is what makes it a team and not just a crowd.

In a team game, the player with the ball is doing the obvious job. Everybody
else is doing a job too:

Moving into a space where a pass can reach them.

Watching the player who is not being watched by anyone else.

Saying something useful and short, like a name or "behind you".

Waiting for their turn without sulking, which is harder than it sounds.

A team where one player does everything is usually losing, and definitely not
enjoying itself.

## Sports in our community

**LOOK AROUND**

Where can people play sport near you? Count the places. A pitch counts. So
does a park, a court, a pool, a hall and a flat stretch of pavement with a wall.

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

## Your community is the people who live and work near you. Sport in a
community needs three things: somewhere to play, someone to organise it,
and people who turn up.

Somewhere to play might be a school hall in the evening, a village court, a
municipal pool, or a green space by the river. Some places cost money to use
and some are free, which matters a great deal to who can actually play.

Someone to organise it is often a club, a school, or a group of families who
take turns. Most sport in a small town runs on people doing unpaid jobs
quietly.

And people who turn up. A pitch with nobody on it is not sport, it is a field.

**UNIT 1 - TOPIC 1.8**

## Different people, different sports

**OUT IN THE WORLD**

Bento's shoulder aches in winter, so he throws underarm now. Gonçalo
cannot see far, so his team calls his name before they pass. Leonor is the
smallest, so at skittles she stands two paces closer than everyone else.

Nobody in the garden thinks any of this is unfair. It took them a while to get
there.

**HERE IS HOW IT WORKS**

People's bodies are different, and they change over a lifetime. Sport is for all
of those bodies, not only for the fastest ones.

Some sports are changed a little so more people can play:

A ball with a bell inside it, so players who cannot see can hear where it is.

Basketball played in wheelchairs, with the same hoop at the same height.

Swimming races grouped so that people race others who move in a similar
way.

Standing closer, throwing underarm, or having a runner for someone who
cannot run.

Changing a rule so somebody can join in is not making the game easier. It is
## making the game theirs as well as yours.

**FROM THE POTTING SHED**

The first Paralympic Games were held in Rome in 1960. They now happen in
the same year and the same host city as the Olympic Games.

## For teachers

## The book. Global Perspectives, Year 3, Student Book. Six units, 47 topics from the
scheme of work, three term review spreads, a word list, sources and an answers
section. Written for ages 7 to 8 in British English, with Portuguese contexts, euros,
metric units and the 24 hour clock.

## How a unit runs. Each unit opens with a plate and one big question, then states
what the unit is for. Topics are numbered so a page can be found and cited.
Panels are tools rather than a checklist: a topic takes the two or three it needs, so
the furniture varies from topic to topic on purpose. The panel names and their
## jobs are printed in How to use this book at the front.

## The thread. One question runs through all six units: who is left out, and what
would include them? It appears as a line drawn in the wrong place in Unit 1, as
dignity in Unit 2, as access in Units 3 and 5, as being heard in Unit 4, and as
respect in Unit 6. The Term 3 review makes it explicit.

## What the book assumes. Counting in fours or multiplying by 4, reading a simple
table, using the 24 hour clock, and simple money arithmetic in euros. Topic 1.4
asks pupils to find a pulse; some will not manage it at the neck and should use
the inside of the wrist, or count breaths, which shows the same effect.

Topics needing a word before you start.

## 1.8 discusses adapted sport and disability. The rule taught is to ask about the
## sport, never about somebody's body, and to accept a no.

## 2.1 to 2.6 discuss poverty. The book stays at the level of needs, homes, work
and services, and never asks a pupil to say anything about their own family's
money. "Not sure" is offered as a valid answer throughout.

## 3.5 covers online safety and says plainly that some decisions belong to an
adult. Please make sure pupils know which two adults they would fetch.

## 6.1 and 6.2 discuss families. Every task can be answered about an invented
family or about the book's cast, and the unit says so in print before the first
topic.
