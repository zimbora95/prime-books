Green means start. Red means stop. They look the same in this book as they do on the screen,
so you will know them straight away.

✎For teachers

Before beginning Unit 1, check that pupils can follow a three-step spoken
instruction in order, and can point to left and right on their own body. Both
matter far more to early programming than any vocabulary.

Unit 1 needs no devices at all. If a floor robot is available it is ideal for Unit 1, but
a chalked grid and a child pretending to be Beep works just as well, and pupils
often understand it better.

What is an error?

🔄In the yard today

Tomás said forward four times. Beep only needed three.

So Beep went forward four times, because that is what Tomás

said. And on the fourth one, Beep rolled gently into a cardboard

box and stopped.

Beep did nothing wrong. Beep did exactly what it was told.

💡Here is how it works

## An error is a step that does not do what you wanted. The robot

## is never wrong. The instruction was wrong. That is good news,

because you can change an instruction.

🎫Chip's fact card

## Programmers call a mistake in an algorithm a bug. In 1947 a team

working on a huge early computer found a real moth stuck inside

it, taped it into their notebook, and wrote that they had been

## debugging the machine. The name stuck.

FOR TEACHERS

Teaching Year 1 computing

The yard set out before a lesson. Most of it needs no devices.

✎For teachers

**This book follows the Prime School scheme of work exactly. Five units, 31**
topics, in the order the scheme sets them out. The unit titles in the running heads
are the scheme's own, so you can map any page to the scheme in one glance. The
yard names are for the pupils.

**Unplugged first, every time. Around half the tasks in this book carry a green No**
**screen needed flag. That is not a fallback for when the tablets are charging.**
Sequencing, spotting an error and fixing one step are learnt far better with cards
on the floor than with a mouse, and pupils who have done it with their bodies
transfer to the screen quickly.

**Debugging is editing, not restarting. The commonest thing you will see is a pupil**
sweeping all their cards away and beginning again. Ask, every time: which is the
FIRST card that is wrong? Then change only that one. This single habit is the most
valuable thing in Unit 1.

✎For teachers · the two subject points

**The block colours in this book are the real ones. They are taken from the**
Scratch editor itself, so a pupil who has met an orange Control block on paper will
reach for the orange drawer on screen. Please do not recolour them in your own
materials.

**Charts must add up. In Unit 3, always have pupils check their survey total**
against the class register before they draw anything. Finding that the total is
wrong, and then finding out why, is the lesson.

FOR TEACHERS

What you need, and what you do not

✎For teachers

**You do not need a floor robot. Unit 1 works with a chalked grid and one child**
pretending to be Beep, and in some ways it works better: a child robot can be
visibly, comically literal in a way a real one cannot. If you do have a floor robot,
Unit 1 is the natural place for it.

**You do not need one device each. Unit 2 works with one screen and a class**
gathered round it, with pupils taking turns to place a block. A two-minute rota
with a visible timer works far better than asking pupils to judge when their turn is
over.

**What you do need: chalk or floor tiles for a grid; a stack of blank cards; coloured**
bricks or buttons for sorting; wool or string; cardboard boxes; and one screen with
Scratch on it. All of it is ordinary classroom stock.

✎For teachers · tone

**Beep is doing the teaching. Every joke in this book has the same shape: Beep**
does exactly what was said, not what was meant, and the result is funny. Lean
into it. Play Beep yourself, in front of the class, and be gloriously literal. Pupils
correct you eagerly, and correcting an instruction is precisely the skill being
taught.

**Two blame-free rules. In Unit 4, telling an adult about anything worrying on a**
screen must never carry consequences. In Unit 5, reporting damage to a device
must never carry consequences either. Pupils who fear the consequence stop
disclosing, which defeats the purpose of both pages.

## If you only have time for one thing from this book, do Bug of the

minutes a week, and it teaches more than an hour of vocabulary.
