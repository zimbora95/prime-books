## 1.1 Introduction

Futsal grew in Uruguay and Brazil in the 1930s as a way to play football in small indoor spaces. FIFA
now governs the international game. The smaller court and the low-bounce size 4 ball force you to
control the ball close to your feet.

A standard school match is five players including a goalkeeper. There is no offside. Restarts are usually
kick-ins, not throw-ins. That one rule changes the whole rhythm of the game: the ball stays on the floor
and the next pass has to be ready.

Year 7 futsal is not a smaller copy of outdoor football. It is its own sport. The best players here are the
ones who lift their head, keep the ball, and help a teammate who has just lost it.

Futsal court (typical school: about 28–40 m × 16–20 m)

**KEY POINTS**

Five outfield roles plus a goalkeeper, rotating often.
Low-bounce ball: first touch must kill the bounce.

Kick-in, not throw-in. Four seconds to restart in the full game.

Fair play and safe footwear on a hard court.

## 1.2 Warm-ups

A futsal warm-up does two jobs. It raises heart rate and it rehearses the shapes you will use in the
lesson: short passing, changes of direction, and stopping under control.

Start with easy jogging and dynamic mobility for ankles, hips and shoulders. Then add ball work while
you are still talking. If the first touch of the lesson is a full-speed shot, the session has started too late.

Warm-up movement should look like the game: short steps, head up, body ready to change direction.

**KEY POINTS**

Pulse raiser: 3 to 4 minutes of easy movement.

Dynamic stretches: open the hips and wake the calves.

Ball activation: pairs passing on the move.

A short reaction game before the main drill.

## 1.3 Ball control

First touch is the skill that buys you time. Receive the ball with a soft surface, usually the inside of the
foot, and send it into the space you already chose. A heavy first touch turns you into a defender.

Dribbling in futsal is done in a tight box. Use the sole to stop and roll, the inside and outside to change
direction, and the sole again to hide the ball from a close marker. Keep the ball within a step.

A good first touch puts the ball where your next step already wants to go.

**KEY POINTS**

Cushion the ball. Do not stab at it.

Take the first touch away from the incoming press.

Sole, inside, outside: three tools, one close ball.

Head up between touches.

## 1.4 Passing

Passing in futsal is short, early and often. The inside-foot pass is the base. Add the sole pass when the
ball is under you and a driven pass along the floor when you must beat a line of pressure.

The receiver is part of the pass. Show a clear target, call for the ball, and move again as soon as it
leaves your teammate. Standing still after a pass is how attacks die.

Passing lanes appear and disappear quickly. The player without the ball creates the next one.

**KEY POINTS**

Inside foot for accuracy, ankle locked.

Pass to the safe foot, away from the marker.

Move after you pass: give an angle.

Use both feet. The court is too small for a favourite side only.

## 1.5 Shooting and finishing

Finishing in futsal is often from a crowded area. Accuracy beats wild power. The laces drive is for a
clear sight of goal. The inside-foot placement is for the far corner when the goalkeeper has stepped
out.

Set your standing foot beside the ball, keep your head still, and follow through toward the target.
Practise with both feet. A Year 7 player who can finish on the weaker side is already hard to defend.

A composed finish starts with the standing foot and a still head, not with a longer backswing.

**KEY POINTS**

Pick a target before you swing.

Low shots are harder for the goalkeeper on a hard court.

Follow in. Rebounds are part of the shot.

Do not shoot when a simple pass creates a better chance.

## 1.6 Goalkeeping

The futsal goalkeeper is a field player with hands in a small area. Stance is low, hands ready, feet ready
to close the angle. Distribution matters as much as the save: a calm roll to a free teammate starts the
next attack.

Every outfield player should spend time in goal during this unit. Understanding the goalkeeper's
picture makes you a more honest attacker.

The goal is small, so positioning beats spectacular diving.

**KEY POINTS**

Ready stance: knees bent, weight on the front of the feet.

Get big. Do not dive early.

Distribute with the hands or a short roll when you can.

Communicate: left, right, time, keeper.

## 1.7 Attacking strategies

Attacks in futsal are built on width, depth and a spare player. One player holds, one supports
underneath, one stretches the far side. The fourth outfield player reads whether to join or to balance.

A common Year 7 pattern is pass and move into the space behind the first defender. Do not all run
toward the ball. The court is already small.

Attacks breathe when players occupy different lines, not the same square metre.

**KEY POINTS**

Create a diamond, not a crowd.

Use the pivot as a back-to-goal link.

Switch play when one side is blocked.

Shoot when the extra player is free, not two passes later.

## 1.8 Defensive strategies

Defence starts the moment you lose the ball. The nearest player presses. The next player covers the
passing line. The rest of the team drops into a compact shape between the ball and the goal.

Marking can be player-to-player or zonal in a simple 2-2. In Year 7, the rule is this: if your player receives
the ball facing your goal, you arrived too late.

A compact defensive shape leaves no easy split pass through the middle.

**KEY POINTS**

Press, cover, balance.

Show the attacker onto their weaker foot.

Do not dive in. Force them sideways.

Talk: I have the ball, you have the next pass.

## 1.9 Game scenarios

Match play is where skills become decisions. Play 3 v 3 and 4 v 4 before a full 5 v 5. Give each game a
constraint so the lesson has a focus: two-touch, weaker-foot finish, or a pass before a shot.

Rotate roles. The quiet player on the wing this game is the pivot next game. Futsal teaching fails if the
same three students take every touch.

A small-sided futsal game: one ball, one goal, everyone involved, no extra instructions in the air.

**KEY POINTS**

Small-sided first, then 5 v 5.

One constraint per game, not five.

Everyone plays. Everyone keeps.

Stop the game to coach one picture, then play on.

## 2.1 Introduction and rules

Cross-country running is racing over natural ground: grass, trails, hills and sometimes mud. It has
been part of the modern Olympic programme and remains a World Athletics discipline. In school it is
also a training method for endurance and resilience.

The rules are simple and strict. You follow the marked course. You do not cut corners. You give other
runners space at the start. You help a fallen runner if they are hurt, then report it.

The course, not the stopwatch, is the first thing to read. Where is the climb? Where can you recover?
Where is the ground uneven?

The trail is the classroom. Read the ground before you race it.

**KEY POINTS**

Stay on the marked route.

Mass start: controlled, not a sprint into a bottleneck.

Team events often score by adding finishing places.

Kit: suitable shoes, no headphones, water before and after.

## 2.3 Running techniques

Efficient running is quiet. Head stacked over shoulders, shoulders over hips. Arms swing from the
shoulder, not the elbow. Cadence is quick enough that you are not overstriding and braking with every
heel.

Uphill: shorter steps, slight lean from the ankles, eyes on a point ahead. Downhill: let the slope help,
do not throw the brakes on, keep the feet under you.

Side-on, a balanced runner looks stacked: head, shoulders, hips and a compact arm swing.

**KEY POINTS**

Tall posture, relaxed face and hands.

Arms drive the rhythm.

Shorten the stride uphill.

Keep the feet under the body on descents.

## 2.5 Race strategies

A race has three parts: a controlled start, a patient middle, and an honest finish. The middle is where
most Year 7 races are won, because that is where people who started too fast come back to you.

In a team race, know whether you are a front runner or a pack runner. Helping a teammate hold a
rhythm can beat chasing a rival alone.

Landmarks on the course are better pacers than the runners immediately in front of you.

**KEY POINTS**

First minute: find space and settle.

Middle: even effort, not even speed. Hills cost more.

Last 300 m: lift the arms, keep the form.

After the line: keep moving, then check on teammates.

## 3.1 Introduction and rules

Basketball was designed in 1891 by James Naismith in Springfield, Massachusetts, as an indoor winter
game. Five players on each side score by shooting the ball through a horizontal ring. FIBA and school
rules share the same spine: travelling, double dribble, fouls and out of bounds.

Scoring is usually two points from the field, three from beyond the arc, and one from the free-throw
line. The game restarts with a throw-in or a free throw after a foul.

Positions have names (guard, forward, centre) but Year 7 players should learn to play more than one.
The court is a shared problem, not five private jobs.

Basketball court: key, centre circle, sideline and baseline

**KEY POINTS**

Five v five. Dribble, pass or shoot. No run with the ball.

A foul is illegal contact that disadvantages an opponent.

Know the key, the three-point line, the sideline and the baseline.

Stop on the whistle. The ball is dead.

## 3.3 Ball handling

Ball handling is two hands and two eyes. The dribble lives at hip height on the move and lower when a

Crossovers, retreat dribbles and a simple spin are enough for Year 7. The aim is to keep the ball when

Eyes up and a protected dribble turn a 1 v 1 into a passing chance.

**KEY POINTS**

Fingertips. Waist or lower.

Protect with the body and the off arm, without pushing.

Change of pace beats empty change of direction.

If you pick the ball up, you must pass or shoot.

## 3.4 Passing

The chest pass, bounce pass and overhead pass cover almost every Year 7 situation. Step toward the
target, finish the hands toward the target, and hit the receiver where they can catch without travelling.

A bounce pass is for traffic. An overhead pass is for a longer skip. A chest pass is the default when the
lane is clear.

A clean passing lane is usually created by the player without the ball.

**KEY POINTS**

Two hands, step, snap.

Pass away from the defender.

Meet the ball. Do not wait with stiff arms.

Call the receiver's name.

## 3.5 Shooting

Shooting is balance plus a repeatable path. Feet shoulder-width, shooting elbow under the ball,
follow through as if reaching into the ring. The layup is a running shot: right hand on the right side, left
on the left, off the correct foot.

Free throws are a routine. Same breaths, same starting spot, every time. Pressure is just a familiar
action in a quieter gym.

## 3.6 Defence

Defence is stance, position and talk. Sit in a wide base, mirror the ball, and stay between your player

**KEY POINTS**

Force the attacker toward the sideline or their weaker hand.
Help and recover.

Box out after every shot.

## 4.1 Introduction and rules

Badminton is a racket sport governed internationally by the BWF. Rally scoring means every rally wins a
point. Games are usually to 21, and you must win by two points. A match is the best of three games.

Singles uses the long, narrow court. Doubles uses the wide court, and the service box changes. The
server starts from the right when their score is even.

The shuttle must be hit before it touches the floor. A serve is underarm and below the ribcage in school
play. Honesty about line calls is part of the sport.

Badminton: singles is the inner side lines; doubles uses the outer

**KEY POINTS**

Rally point to 21, win by two.

Singles and doubles use different lines.

Serve underarm, diagonally.

Call fairly. Replay if you honestly cannot see.

Drive: flat, through the mid-court.

Serve: legal, then immediately ready.

## 4.5 Attacking strokes

## 5.3 Ball control

The forearm pass (bump) uses a flat platform: elbows straight, thumbs together, angle the platform
toward the target. Move the feet first. The arms are a surface, not a swing.

The overhead set uses both hands above the forehead, a quick soft contact, and an even push. The
setter faces the attacker they want to use.

A forearm platform is flat, still and aimed. The legs do the work.

**KEY POINTS**

Feet to the ball.

Platform toward the target.

Set from the forehead, not the chest.

Call early.

## 5.4 Attacking skills

Attacking is approach, jump, contact, land. A three-step approach is enough in Year 7. Contact the ball
in front of the hitting shoulder. Aim for space, not for the defender's chest every time.

A tip or roll shot is still an attack. So is a well-placed down-ball when the set is low.

An attack is timed to the set. The last step plants, the arm is already up.

**KEY POINTS**

Approach in rhythm.

High contact, open hand.
Aim for space.

Land on two feet when you can.

## 5.5 Defensive skills

Defence is reading and a low ready position. On serve receive, angle the platform to the setter. On a
hard attack, get the last contact up, not pretty.

Blocking is timing plus a straight reach over the net without touching it. Year 7 blocks are often about
taking away one angle rather than stuffing every hit.

A low ready position turns a hard serve into a pass instead of a rebound off the chest.

**KEY POINTS**

Ready position before the hitter jumps.

Serve receive to the setter, not just 'up'.

Block the most likely angle.

Cover the hitter on your own team.

## 5.6 Game strategies

A simple offence is pass-set-hit. A simple defence is three at the net or a 4-2 with two setters. Year 7
teams should pick one system and keep it for a block of lessons.

The free ball (a gentle return) is the moment to run the proper offence. Talk: 'free' so everyone knows.

A designated setter turns three random contacts into an offence.

**KEY POINTS**

One system, repeated.

Setter calls the attacker.
Free ball: run the real offence.

Serve to a seam, not always to the same person.

## 6.1 Introduction and rules

Sports aerobics is a judged fitness sport under the FIG family of gymnastics. In school it is a way to
train rhythm, strength, flexibility and performance without needing a full apparatus hall.

Safety is the first rule: land softly, keep knees in line with toes, and never force a flexibility shape. Music
is a partner, not a race.

Routines are short. Quality of shape and timing beats a long sequence that falls apart.

A clear floor, a speaker and enough space: the equipment list is short on purpose.

**KEY POINTS**

Control first, then speed.

Land softly, joints stacked.

Music has counts. Learn them.

Space: know your personal square.

**KEY POINTS**

Clear feet, clear arms.

Both leads.
Stay on the count.

Finish each shape.

## 6.4 Strength elements

Strength elements in school aerobics are controlled: a strong plank, a clean press-up, a tucked jump, a
controlled squat. They are held or landed, not flung.

If you cannot do a full press-up, use a box or knee variation with the same body line. The line is the
skill.

Light equipment is enough. The strength element is the body line.

**KEY POINTS**

Stacked joints.

A hold is still a performance.

Land, then smile. Do not collapse.

Choose the variation you can control.

Warm before you hold.

Level hips on kicks.
Breathe in the hold.

No bouncing into pain.

## 6.6 Routine building

A Year 7 routine is 32 to 64 counts: a start shape, two or three travelling phrases, one strength or
flexibility moment, and a finish shape that you actually hold.

Write it down. Counts on paper prevent arguments in the group and help you rehearse without the
speaker.

Choreography on paper looks unromantic. It is how groups stay together.

**KEY POINTS**

Start and finish are part of the dance.

Travel, then a highlight, then a close.

Write the counts.

Leave space between bodies.

## 6.7 Performance

Performance is the routine plus the way you enter, face the audience and hold the last shape. Eyes up.
A mistake that you continue from is better than a mistake that you narrate with your face.

In a group, the performance is the agreement: same count, same spacing, same energy on the
highlight.

A group performance is timing plus spacing. The highlight only works if both are shared.

**KEY POINTS**

Enter on purpose.

Recover from errors without stopping.

Hold the last shape.

Applaud other groups as you want to be applauded.

## Keep moving after the last lesson

Physical Education is a school subject. Physical activity is a life habit. The two only meet if you choose
them to. You do not need a club trial to stay active. You need a plan you will actually keep.

**IN SCHOOL**

Join a lunch club. Offer to referee. Help set up the
next class.

**OUT OF SCHOOL**

Walk or cycle a regular route. Play a small-sided
game in the park.

**WITH OTHERS**

Invite someone who does not usually play. Teach
them one skill from this book.

**ON YOUR OWN**

Ten minutes of mobility or an easy run still counts.
Write it down.

**A FAIR TARGET**
Pick one session a week that is not a PE lesson. Put it on a calendar. If you miss it, restart the next
week. Do not wait for September.

The six sports in this book will return, in harder forms, in Years 8 and 9. The students who enjoy those
years are usually the ones who kept the simple habits: a decent warm-up, an honest first touch, and a
word for the person next to them.

## Word list

These words are used in the units. The definition matches the way the book uses the word.

**Agility**
The ability to change direction quickly and under control.

**Attack line**
The three-metre line in volleyball that limits back-row attacks.

**Base**
The recovery position on a badminton court, usually near the centre in singles.

**Clear**
A high, deep badminton shot to the back of the court.

**Cool-down**
Easy movement and stretching that bring the pulse down after work.

**Extra player**
The attacker who is free because teammates have drawn defenders away.

**FIG**
Fédération Internationale de Gymnastique, the international gymnastics federation.

**First touch**
The first contact that receives a moving ball and sets up the next action.

**Free ball**
A gentle volleyball return that lets the receiving team run a full offence.

**Help and recover**
Leaving your player briefly to stop a drive, then returning.

**Pivot**
A futsal attacker who plays with their back to goal and links play.

**Platform**
The flat surface made by the forearms in a volleyball pass.

**Rally scoring**
A system in which every rally wins a point, not only the serving side.

**Split step**
A small hop just as the opponent hits, so you can push off in any direction.

**Talk test**
A simple check: if you can speak a sentence, the endurance pace is about right.

**Travelling**
In basketball, taking too many steps without dribbling.

## For teachers

This Student Book covers the Year 7 Physical Education course as mapped from the department source
file: Futsal, Cross Country, Basketball, Badminton, Volleyball and Sports Aerobics. Each unit keeps the
source topic list. Teaching prose is original.

Assessment pages match the source policy: collective game assessment, three fitness tests, and a
written paper when practical evidence is missing. Term dates and event days, if the school names them
later, can be announced in class. They are not printed here as if they were already fixed.

Coach's tips are written in a staff voice without naming individual teachers. The printed title is Physical
Education, Year 7.

Body type is 12 pt Source Sans 3. Do not shrink the type to fit a lesson onto fewer pages. Split the task
