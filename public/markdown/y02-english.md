FOR TEACHERS

This book assumes four to five hours of English a week, which is roughly a unit every three and a half
weeks across a 34-week year, with time left over for reading for pleasure.

Each unit is built to be taught in order: the reading text comes first because every skill afterwards is
taught out of it. The practice pages can be split across lessons, and the project is designed to run over
two or three.

Pupils need the word slips from the first week. They are the book's one piece of continuous apparatus,
and Units 3, 5 and 8 all depend on a jar that has been filling up since September.

Answers are not printed. Where a task has one right answer the modelled attempt shows how it is
reached, and the reasoning prompts are meant to be talked through rather than marked.

SPEECH MARKS, AND A NEW LINE FOR EACH SPEAKER

## Speech marks go around the exact words somebody says, and
## nothing else. When a different person speaks, you start a new
## line.

## A comma is the small mark that separates one part of a
sentence from another. In speech it goes inside the speech
marks, just before the closing one.

## “It is a bit wonky,” said Ines.

The speech marks hold only the words that came out of her mouth. The words said Ines are outside
them, because nobody said those. The comma goes inside.

WHAT HAPPENED AT PRIME SCHOOL
Tomas wrote a whole page with no speech marks in it and nobody
could tell who was talking.

He read it out and it made sense, because he knew who said what.
Then Aisha read it out and got it wrong twice. That is what speech
marks are for: they are not decoration, they tell a reader where a
voice starts and stops.

## Being careful is not being frightened. Water is one of the best

things there is. Almost everybody who reads this will spend a happy

day beside some this year. Being safe near it is not about being afraid

of it: it is about knowing four or five things, so that you can stop

thinking about them and enjoy yourself.

## 2Swim where somebody is watching. The single most useful rule is to

swim where there is a lifeguard, and to swim with somebody rather

than alone. A lifeguard is not there because swimming is dangerous. A

lifeguard is there because somebody watching the water is the thing

that most often makes a difference.

## 3Cold water is the surprise. Here is the part most people do not

know. Cold water does not only feel unpleasant: it makes your muscles

stop working properly, quite quickly, and it does this to strong

swimmers exactly as it does to weak ones. Being good at swimming

does not protect you from it. That is why the sea in a cold country can

be difficult even on a hot day, because the air being warm tells you

nothing at all about the water.

Safe near water continued

## If you fall in, float first. If you go into cold water without meaning

to, the advice is to float on your back, keep still, and let your

breathing settle before you try to do anything else. Fighting the water

is what uses you up. Once your breathing is calm again you can shout

for help or swim.

## A life ring is thrown, never carried. If somebody else is in trouble,

the rule is to tell an adult and to throw something that floats. You do

not go in after them. That sounds hard, and it is: it is also the advice

every lifeboat service in the world gives, because two people in the

water is worse than one.

## Signs and flags are worth reading. A flag or a sign at a beach or a

river tells you where it is safe and where it is not, and somebody who

knows that stretch of water put it there this morning. Reading it takes

four seconds.

AN ORIGINAL STORY · PRIME BOOKS · PART ONE

Rui was not tired. He wanted everybody to know it. He had said so

at half past seven, and again at eight, and he was still saying it at

half past eight from the top of the stairs.

2“I am not tired,” he called down. “I have never been less tired in my

life.”

“Bed,” said his mother, without looking up.

3So Rui went to his room, but he did not get into bed. He got his torch

instead, and he sat on the floor and built a tower out of wooden

blocks. It was the tallest tower he had ever built. There was nobody

awake to see it.

4The tower fell over. Rui listened. Nobody came.

5He went to the window and pulled the curtain back. Outside, the

garden had gone a strange grey-blue, and everything in it was the

wrong colour. The washing line was still there. The shed was still

there. On the line, where no bird sits in the daytime, there was an owl.

The Night Rui Would Not Sleep

PART TWO

It turned its head all the way round and looked at Rui. Rui looked

back. Neither of them moved for a very long time.

“I am not tired either,” the owl did not say, because owls do not talk.

But Rui thought it anyway, and it seemed to him the most sensible

thing anybody had thought all evening.

He stayed at the window while the owl waited. He was still there when

it dropped off the line and went across the garden without a sound,

and he was still there when the grey-blue got darker and the shed

stopped being a shed and became a shape.

Then Rui was cold. He got into bed to get warm, only to get warm, and

he was asleep before he had finished thinking about how he was not

going to fall asleep.

In the morning his mother found him with one arm out of the covers

and the torch still on, shining at nothing under the blanket, and the

fallen tower all over the floor. She stood in the doorway and laughed

quietly so as not to wake him, because now, of course, he was very

hard to wake indeed.

HOW TO LEARN A POEM BY HEART

1
Read the whole poem out loud once, so you know where it is
going.

2
Learn the first verse only. Say it until you can do it without
looking.

3
Add the second verse to the first. Always say it from the
beginning.

4
Keep adding one verse at a time. Never start in the middle.

5
Say it to somebody. That is different from saying it to yourself,
and it is the part that makes it stick.

They came out slowly. They went up past her face and up past the

shelves and they spread across the low wooden ceiling of the shed

until the whole of it was a map of stars, and Aisha lay down on the

floor among the flowerpots to look at it properly.

She stayed there a long time. Then she thought about her brother,

who was four, and who was frightened of his bedroom at night, and

who had told her so and asked her not to tell anybody.

It took her most of the evening to get them back in. She had to stand

very still and wait, and let them come to the jar in their own time, and

it was almost dark outside when she screwed the lid down again.

She did not wrap it. She carried it up and knocked on his door with her

elbow because both her hands were full.

“It is not a present,” she said. “You have to give it to somebody else

afterwards. That is the rule.”

Her brother took it with both hands. He did not ask who made the

rule.

INFORMATION TEXT · EMERGENCY NUMBER CORRECT FOR PORTUGAL

## What the job really is. Most people think a firefighter spends the

day putting out fires. In fact a modern fire and rescue service is

called out to far more than fires: to road accidents, to floods, to

people trapped in lifts and machinery, and to animals stuck where

they should not be. Fires are the part everybody knows about, and

they are only part of the work.

## 2Before the call comes. A great deal of the job happens when nothing

is happening. Equipment is checked every single shift, because a hose

that fails at a fire fails at the worst possible moment. Firefighters

train constantly, they walk round buildings in their area so they

already know the way in, and they visit schools, because a fire that

never starts is better than one that is put out.

## 3What they wear, and why. The heavy coat and trousers are not just

thick: they are made in layers, with an outer layer that resists flame

and an inner layer that keeps steam away from the skin. The helmet

has a visor. The boots have a steel plate in the sole, because the floor

of a burnt building is full of nails. Breathing apparatus lets a

firefighter go into smoke, and smoke, not flame, is what makes a fire

so dangerous.
