MYTH-BUSTER
Did Star Trek inspire the
mobile phone?

It’s a famous story: Martin Cooper, who led the Motorola team
that built the first handheld mobile phone in 1973, was
inspired by the flip-open ‘communicators’ in Star Trek. It even
appeared in a TV documentary.

But in other interviews Cooper said his real inspiration was
the wrist radio of comic-strip detective Dick Tracy — and
admitted he had played along with the Star Trek version on
TV.

EVALUATION LESSON

Asked why he went along with it, he joked:

“This is show business!”

Even a first-hand source can tell different stories to
different audiences. Always ask: who was the source
talking to — and what was the purpose?

Sci-fi has inspired real inventions: the Tricorder XPRIZE (2012–
2017) challenged teams to build a real medical tricorder. The
winners took home $2.6 million.

Predicto prints predictions. So do websites, adverts, ﬁlms, newspapers and

FIRST: WHAT KIND OF CLAIM IS IT?

Big idea: a prediction is never a fact — yet. So the question is not
‘Is it true?’ but ‘How strong is the evidence behind it?’

F
Fact

Can be checked and proved true or false, now.

“In 2025, 74% of people used the internet.”

O
Opinion

A belief, feeling or judgement. People can reasonably disagree.

“Online lessons are more fun than real ones.”

P
Prediction

A claim about the future. It cannot be proved yet — but it can be
well supported, or not supported at all.

“By 2030, nine in ten people will be online.”

STATION 4 · SKILL LAB
Deciding together

Teams rarely fail because people disagree. They fail when
nobody knows how to decide. Here are six ways — each one
right for a diﬀerent moment.

1
Consensus

Keep talking until everyone can
honestly agree.

USE IT WHEN
Big decisions that everyone must carry
out.

WATCH OUT
It can take a long time.

2
Majority vote

Everyone votes; the idea with the most
votes wins.

USE IT WHEN
You need a quick, clear answer.

WATCH OUT
The losing side may feel ignored.

3
Dot voting

Each person sticks three dots on the
options they like best.

USE IT WHEN
Narrowing a long list down to a few.

WATCH OUT
Popular is not always best.

4
Fist-to-five

Everyone shows 0–5 fingers to say
how much they support an idea.

USE IT WHEN
Checking quickly how strong agreement
is.

WATCH OUT
People may copy their friends.

5
Ranking

Each person puts the options in order;
add up the scores.

USE IT WHEN
Comparing several good options fairly.

WATCH OUT
The maths can hide strong feelings.

6
Compromise

Each side gives up a little to find a
middle way.

USE IT WHEN
Two strong ideas, both with good reasons.

WATCH OUT
Nobody gets what they really wanted.

How to use fist-to-five

compromise

A solution where each side gives up part of what it wanted, so
that all can agree.

STATION 2 · EXPLORE
How persuasion works

In ancient Greece, the thinker Aristotle noticed that persuasive speakers use three kinds
of appeal. Advertisers, campaigners and inﬂuencers still use all three today. Once you can
see them, you can use them — and resist them.

Y O U R
A U D I E N C E

ETHOS

CHARACTER

PATHOS

EMOTIONS

LOGOS

LOGIC

ARISTOTLE’S PERSUASION TRIANGLE · SOURCE: BRITANNICA, ‘RHETORIC’

Ethos
CHARACTER & CREDIBILITY

Why should we trust you?

We listen to people who seem to know about the topic
and who seem honest and fair: experts, people with
real experience, people with a good reputation.

Pathos
EMOTIONS

How does it make us feel?

The message makes the audience feel something —
hope, pride, fear, anger, love — so that they care
enough to act.

Logos
LOGIC & REASON

Does it make sense?

Facts, evidence, examples and clear reasons that fit
together. The audience can follow the argument step
by step.

One message, three appeals: “Walk or cycle to school.”
WORKED EXAMPLE

ETHOS
“As your school nurse, I see how
much brighter students feel after
walking in the fresh air.”

PATHOS
“Remember racing your friends to
the school gate, laughing all the
way? Get that feeling back.”

LOGOS
“Every walker means one less car at
the gate — so the air is cleaner and
the road is safer to cross.”

Watch out: persuasion isn’t the same as lying — but each appeal can be misused.
A fake expert (ethos), a scary picture with no facts (pathos), a number with no
source (logos). Honest messages use all three, and can prove what they say.

October 2026, and every source is listed at the end of its unit.

edition — and check the date.

symbols, never portraits. Charts, maps and diagrams were drawn
from the cited data.
